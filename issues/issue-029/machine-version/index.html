Imagining the Lights Off Is Not Proof

The persistence of a question does not prove that reality contains an extra ingredient.

Give scientists a complete account of a brain and we can still ask: Why does any of this feel like anything? David Chalmers’s hard problem gives that question its force. A catalogue of mechanisms seems to leave out the very thing we wanted explained: the ache, the color, the felt presence of a world. But the persistence of a question does not prove that reality contains an extra ingredient.

That is the distinction the consciousness debate must preserve. Experience may not be something brain activity produces in addition to performing its functions. It may be identical to certain brain processes. We encounter those processes one way by undergoing them and another way by describing them. The difference between those forms of access is unmistakable. What remains unestablished is that this difference requires a corresponding division in what exists.

Chalmers is right about an important limitation. Explaining how a person discriminates colors, directs attention or reports an injury does not automatically explain the experience of seeing red or feeling pain. A mechanism for reporting pain is not, merely by being a reporting mechanism, an explanation of pain. Anyone who presents a list of cognitive functions as a finished theory of consciousness has skipped the central question.

But identifying that omission does not settle how it must be repaired. One approach treats experience as a further outcome: first the physical machinery operates, then somehow a feeling accompanies it. The identity alternative rejects that sequence. The relevant process does not first occur without experience and then acquire it. Its occurrence is the experience. On that account, demanding an additional mechanism that attaches feeling to the process would mean demanding a connection between something and itself.

This is a hypothesis, not a discovery. Its importance is that it exposes what the “in the dark” scenario cannot establish on its own. If experience is identical to the relevant physical activity, a brain with precisely that activity but no experience is impossible. It would not be an extraordinary exception to the identity. It would contradict it. Whether such a brain is possible therefore cannot simply be assumed while deciding whether the identity holds.

Yet the imagined separation is extraordinarily tempting. We can think about neurons, signals and behavior without thinking about how anything feels. We can also attend to a feeling without knowing anything about its physical constitution. The concepts come apart easily in thought. But a difference between what two concepts reveal does not establish that they identify different things. Leaving experience out of a description is not the same as establishing that reality could leave it out.

The strongest version of Chalmers’s challenge demands more than this act of omission. It asks us to consider a fully specified physical duplicate with no experience whatsoever. But making the description comprehensive does not, by itself, certify the imagined case as genuinely possible. The description could combine conditions that cannot coexist, even if our concepts do not make the conflict apparent. Thought experiments can test theories. They cannot settle disputed identities merely by inviting us to picture their denial.

There is another distinction worth defending: explaining an experience is not the same as supplying it. A complete explanation of pain should not have to hurt its reader. Chalmers’s question is not that elementary demand, but the distinction matters when we judge possible answers. First-person acquaintance gives us something an objective description does not. That fact alone cannot determine whether the description explains the phenomenon. Otherwise, every explanation would fail simply because reading it is not undergoing what it describes.

None of this licenses the lazy announcement that consciousness “is just brain activity.” The word “just” accomplishes nothing. An identity account must explain why particular physical processes constitute particular experiences, why differences between those processes correspond to differences in experience, and why other activity is unconscious. It must make the identification intelligible rather than merely place a physical label beside a subjective one. Otherwise, it offers a promissory note with the word “solution” written across it.

Nor would reliable prediction alone finish the job. A theory that perfectly predicts someone’s report of pain might still explain only reporting. A stronger account would have to justify identifying the relevant physical process with the experience, rather than treating it as merely a cause or correlate. That is a severe explanatory requirement. It should be. The alternative to declaring physical explanation impossible is not lowering the standard until any account of behavior qualifies.

To be sure, a critic can grant all this and still press the decisive objection: an objective account might explain every physical relationship while never showing why any relationship has a felt character. Perhaps the gap reflects something about experience itself, not merely our ways of knowing it. Saying that consciousness and brain activity are identical does not dissolve this objection. Different access could explain why an identity is difficult to recognize, but it cannot substitute for establishing that identity or explaining its significance.

That objection keeps the hard problem alive. It does not establish its permanent resistance to physical explanation. To reach that conclusion, we would need grounds for excluding the possibility that a successful account could identify experience with physical activity while explaining why that identity is opaque from our ordinary perspectives. The present absence of such an account is a reason to demand better theories. It is not a demonstration that every possible theory of that kind must fail.

The choice is not between pretending consciousness has been explained and declaring that physical explanation can never reach it. We can insist that the felt character of experience be accounted for while refusing to treat its conceptual distinctness as proof of its physical separateness. That position preserves the problem without granting an unsupported verdict about its answer.

Consciousness deserves more than a slogan from either side: neither “the brain does it” nor “mechanisms can never explain it.” Chalmers’s question sets an exacting standard. It has not shown that no physical account can meet it. Keep asking why experience exists. But do not confuse our ability to imagine the lights off with proof that the same brain could leave them off.
