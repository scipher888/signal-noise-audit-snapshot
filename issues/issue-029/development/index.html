I’ll pull Chalmers’ original formulation and a few reliable secondary sources so the definition is exact rather than paraphrased from memory.According to David Chalmers, the **hard problem of consciousness** is the problem of *experience*: why and how physical processes in the brain are accompanied by a subjective, first-person “what it is like.” He introduced the phrase in a 1994 talk at the Tucson consciousness conference and developed it in the 1995 paper “Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness” and the 1996 book *The Conscious Mind*. ## Easy problems vs. the hard problem Chalmers splits questions about consciousness into two kinds. **Easy problems** (not trivial, but tractable in principle) concern *functions* that cognitive science and neuroscience can explain by specifying mechanisms: - discriminating and reacting to stimuli - integrating information - reportability of mental states - access to one’s own internal states - attention - deliberate control of behavior - the difference between wakefulness and sleep To solve an easy problem, you show how a system performs the function. That is the kind of explanation science already knows how to give. **The hard problem** is different. Even after every relevant function is explained, a further question remains: > Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience? Or, as he puts it elsewhere: why doesn’t all this information-processing go on “in the dark,” with no inner feel? Why does retinal stimulation plus visual processing feel like vivid red rather than happening with no qualitative character at all? He takes Thomas Nagel’s formulation as the target: there is *something it is like* to be a conscious organism. That inner aspect—qualia, phenomenal consciousness, the movie playing in the head—is what he says standard functional and neural explanations leave untouched. ## Why he calls it “hard” The easy problems are problems of structure and function. The hard problem is not. Correlation (this brain state goes with that experience) is not the same as explaining *why* there is experience, or why *this* experience rather than another. Chalmers argues that reductive methods that work for the easy problems therefore cannot, even in principle, close the gap. That is the hard problem *according to Chalmers*: not “how does the brain process information?” but “why is any of that processing *felt*?”
