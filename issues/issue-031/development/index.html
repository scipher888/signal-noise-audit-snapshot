Essay draft: George Saunders Is Right About Writing. He May Be Wrong About the Rest of Us. AI may well replace the hard thinking that writing demands. For people who would otherwise never write, the trade could still be worth it. When George Saunders sat down with Substack's "Model Behavior" series to talk about artificial intelligence, he asked readers to "imagine me banging my cane on the floor." Then he banged it. He has never tried writing with AI, "not for a second," and wouldn't use it "even in an email." His verdict was blunt: "By my definition of writing, writing with AI isn't. Isn't writing." I write with AI, on purpose. The interview hit hard, because most of what Saunders says is true. His case rests on struggle. Taking a piece from "blurry crap" to "precisely what I mean, and then some," he argues, is how a person discovers what they believe. Skip the struggle and you skip the self. Early research points his way. A 2025 MIT Media Lab study found that participants who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed the weakest brain engagement of any group, and many struggled to quote what they had just "written." The study is small and preliminary. It's still a warning worth heeding. But Saunders is answering a question most people aren't asking. He is defending art: the novel, the short story, the "human-to-human, highly advanced, nuanced code" of literature. Most writing isn't that. It's the grant application, the note to a child's teacher, the memo that decides whether a project lives, the newsletter from someone with something to say and no MFA. For that writing, the alternative to AI isn't Lincoln in the Bardo. It's muddle, or silence. Start with who gets to be legible. Saunders tells students that "language is power," and he's right. But that power has always been rationed. In the most recent national writing assessment, only about a quarter of American eighth- and twelfth-graders wrote at a proficient level. The adults those students became are now nurses, contractors, engineers, caregivers and immigrants writing in a second language. They have ideas worth hearing and no spare decade to master prose. Telling them to struggle through it alone isn't a defense of human expression. It's a defense of the people who already had the time. Now look at where the struggle actually lives. Saunders prints the draft, reads it with a sharp pencil, adjusts "by taste," types in the changes, and does it again, over weeks, months, years. "Thousands of micro-choices" accumulate until his sensibility has "super-permeated the thing." He is chasing a feeling he calls "undeniability," and hoping the story will "overflow its banks." That is where writing is learned. It happens after the first draft, where anyone can spill words, in the revising and revising again until the page brings one's intuition into focus. That is precisely the part a frontier model now does in seconds. Hand it a rough draft and it returns a sharpened version. The writer never has to decide which word, or why. Without real wrestling with the machine, it's unclear how much of the writer survives that pass. So concede the point at full strength. For most people who use it, AI will replace most or all of the hard thinking writing normally requires. Most people won't carefully ration it, or write a draft first out of principle. The honest question is whether the benefits might be worth it anyway. They may be. To be sure, the critics have a point. Competent writing isn't the Olympics. It's basic fitness, and you can't hire someone to do your push-ups. Saunders's deeper fear is cultural. He imagines a world "force-fed Muzak all the time, even in concert halls," until "the aesthetic adjusts downward." Machine-averaged prose, he worries, will dull our taste until we can't tell the difference. That fear underrates readers, and it underrates Saunders. As long as there are writers like him, the downward drift has a ceiling. People are drawn to work that stands apart, especially work that carries a human being inside it. Saunders says AI can't draw on "the experience of having moved through (and heard and smelled and seen and had feelings about) the actual world." If he's right, the gap between his work and the machine's is structural, and readers will keep finding it. It may close only if AI gains a body and lived experience. That is the premise of the story Saunders says he'd like to write. And the Muzak argument looks in only one direction. Saunders tested AI on himself, asking it to imitate his voice, and found the result "dull and literal." For him, that verdict is right. But he asked what happens when someone like him writes with AI. He didn't ask what happens when someone who rarely writes begins to. There, the floor might rise. Economists call it the Jevons paradox: when something gets cheaper, people tend to use more of it, not less. Make writing cheap and more people may write. They may read more, including their own words, revised and returned to them. Some may eventually venture onto the page without help. That is what happened to me. Six months after I started writing with AI, I began writing pieces entirely on my own. One case proves little, but early research echoes it. In a 2024 study of undergraduate ESL students, ChatGPT feedback improved academic writing. In a study of 33 EFL students, it improved grammar, vocabulary and organization, eased anxiety and built confidence. The cost shows up too. Echoing the MIT study, knowledge workers surveyed for a 2025 CHI paper reported spending less cognitive effort with AI. The encouraging studies are small and mostly of language learners. None tracks whether help becomes an on-ramp to writing alone. Even careless use puts opposing views in front of people who would never have sought them. A night-shift nurse who asks a chatbot to sharpen a memo may get, unprompted, an objection she hadn't considered. Amateur writers have had no editor. Now they get a second opinion. It is imperfect, and sometimes flattering, since models tend to agree with their users. In isolation, letting a machine hammer out the words does nothing for anyone's cognitive capability. Still, a clearer cover letter, a sharper report or a persuasive proposal can change someone's job prospects. Those gains can compound into an upward-mobility spiral, whether or not the person's writing skills ever improve one iota. So the danger is the tool. But so is the promise. The same machine that can hollow out a student's education can hand a night-shift nurse the memo that gets her promoted. It can bring a reluctant reader back to the page, or keep him from ever reaching it. Over a lifetime, no one knows how this ends. Anyone who claims certainty, in either direction, is selling something. Saunders closes with Bartleby the Scrivener: "I would prefer not to." It's a fair answer for someone who has spent a lifetime earning his voice, and literature is better for his preferring not to. For most people, the choice is murkier. On one side is a real cognitive cost. On the other is a real chance to be heard, and possibly a road back to writing for oneself. Preferring not to is one answer. Weighing those trade-offs honestly, without pretending we know how they net out, is another. For most of us, it's the more truthful one.

The enemy is the writer who puts down the pencil. --- ## FINAL DRAFT **Title:** George Saunders Drew the Line in the Wrong Place **Word count:** 1128 --- George Saunders warned his interviewer at the outset of his September 30 Model Behavior conversation to imagine him "banging my cane on the floor." Then he banged it. Writing with AI, he said, is "anathema. A thing to be most radically avoided." Not in a novel, not in a story, "not even in an email." It is a bracing performance, and much of it is right. But the most persuasive passage in the interview is not his denunciation. It is his description of how he actually revises. He reads a hard copy "with a sharp pencil in hand, adjusting the text, by taste." Then he does it again, and again, until the story narrows, surprises him, and becomes "about" something he couldn't have stated in advance. "It's the thousands of micro-choices I've made," he writes, "that make the story 'mine'—that imprint my sensibility into the thing. My taste has super-permeated the thing." That is a superb theory of authorship. It is also the best argument against his own verdict. If a story becomes yours through the judgment you exercise over it, then the line that matters isn't between writing with AI and writing without it. It's between keeping your judgment and surrendering it. Saunders has built a case against the second and pronounced sentence on the first. Notice who the villain is in Saunders's argument. It is never a writer weighing, rejecting, and reshaping. It is a writer who hands the job off. "Who are we ceding our authority to, exactly," he asks, "when we let AI write for us?" His mocking catalogue of the new era runs through the instant Rembrandt cat, the album made by someone who can't play, the resume redone in ten seconds, and "I can get a first draft of my novel by giving AI a rough outline!" Every example is wholesale delegation: the machine does the work, the human collects the credit. Against that, his case is devastating. A person who outsources a novel from an outline has skipped the struggle Saunders rightly prizes: "of struggling to say what he wants to say, of making himself evident in his prose, of trying to find out what he believes." No argument here. But then he asks the question that gives the game away: "Did you have a sufficient hand in it?" *Sufficient* implies a threshold, and a threshold implies that some hands are sufficient. You cannot coherently ask whether someone's involvement crossed a line while also insisting that any contact with the tool is anathema. The question is the right one. The verdict skips it. Saunders's revision process clarifies where the threshold sits. What "imprints my sensibility," by his own account, is not the origin of each raw sentence. It is the accumulated pressure of taste: thousands of decisions about sound, rhythm, and what he calls "undeniability," the sense that a reasonable reader is still with him. Those decisions are acts of rejection as much as creation. The pencil kills more than it keeps. A writer who asks a machine for ten versions of a transitional sentence, discards nine, and rewrites the tenth beyond recognition is making exactly that kind of choice. So is a writer who uses a chatbot to argue against her draft, then ignores most of what it says. In both cases the machine is an object of judgment, not its replacement. Whether that's wise in a given instance is a fair question. Nothing Saunders argues shows it is categorically forbidden. His evidence is also thinner than his certainty. His one experiment was to ask AI "to generate some text in my voice," which produced something "so dull and literal" that he lost interest. Asked whether he had tried writing or revising with it, he answered: "I have not. Not for a second." That test proves a machine is a poor counterfeit of George Saunders. Nobody serious was claiming otherwise. It tells us nothing about a writer who never asks the machine to be her, only to give her something to push against. To be sure, Saunders's strongest point deserves full weight. He argues that the messy first draft is where the writer lives. "Your mess has more you in it," he says, seeded with small errors and imprecisions that revision refines into something "utterly you." Editing your own chaos, he says, is "more organic and beneficial" than editing what a machine hands you. He also cites research suggesting that leaning on AI dulls our capabilities, and he is right to worry about a culture that treats ease as the highest good. This is a real cost, and it marks where AI use goes wrong. A writer who never produces her own mess, who starts every piece from the machine's tidy average, is likely to lose exactly what Saunders describes. But look at his language: the mess has *more* you in it. That is a comparison, not a prohibition. It argues for protecting the generative struggle, keeping first drafts human, and staying wary of habit and dependence. It does not support the claim that a single machine-suggested phrase, judged and remade by a working writer, turns her book into someone else's. Writers have always reacted to material they didn't produce: an overheard sentence, a workshop note, a line from another book that lodged in them. Saunders himself says he wants, in fiction, the "subtle record" of every book a writer has read. Influence absorbed and transformed by taste has never been disqualifying. The question was always who was doing the transforming. Collapsing that distinction carries its own danger. Saunders says, "By my definition of writing, writing with AI isn't." If any AI contact disqualifies writing, then a student who uses the tool carefully and one who uses it to fake a Tolstoy essay have committed the same sin. That flattening teaches nothing. Worse, it surrenders the field: if every use is equally damned, there is no reason to learn the difference between using a tool and being used by it. His own standard is far more useful than his anathema, because it tells writers what to protect: a sufficient hand, sustained taste, and choices that make the work "mine." Saunders closes his case by invoking Bartleby: "I [would] prefer not to." That is his right, and for a writer of his gifts it may well be the wise choice. Declining, as he says, is a valid option. But a preference is not a principle, and his principle points somewhere else. Writing worth reading comes from a person whose taste has saturated the work. The enemy was never the machine on the desk. It's the writer who puts down the pencil.
