He May Be Wrong About the Rest of Us. *AI may well replace the hard thinking that writing demands. For people who would otherwise never write, the trade could still be worth it.* When George Saunders sat down with Substack's "Model Behavior" series to talk about artificial intelligence, he asked readers to "imagine me banging my cane on the floor." Isn't writing." I write with AI, on purpose. The interview hit hard, because most of what Saunders says is true. His case rests on struggle. Taking a piece from "blurry crap" to "precisely what I mean, and then some," he argues, is how a person discovers what they believe. Skip the struggle and you skip the self. Early research points his way. A 2025 MIT Media Lab study found that participants who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed the weakest brain engagement of any group, and many struggled to quote what they had just "written." The study is small and preliminary. It's still a warning worth heeding. But Saunders is answering a question most people aren't asking. He is defending art: the novel, the short story, the "human-to-human, highly advanced, nuanced code" of literature. Most writing isn't that. It's the grant application, the note to a child's teacher, the memo that decides whether a project lives, the newsletter from someone with something to say and no MFA. For that writing, the alternative to AI isn't *Lincoln in the Bardo*. It's muddle, or silence. **Start with who gets to be legible.** Saunders tells students that "language is power," and he's right. But that power has always been rationed. In the most recent national writing assessment, only about a quarter of American eighth- and twelfth-graders wrote at a proficient level. The adults those students became are now nurses, contractors, engineers, caregivers and immigrants writing in a second language. They have ideas worth hearing and no spare decade to master prose. Telling them to struggle through it alone isn't a defense of human expression. It's a defense of the people who already had the time. **Now look at where the struggle actually lives.** Saunders describes his method plainly. He prints the draft, reads it with a sharp pencil, adjusts "by taste," types in the changes, and does it again, over weeks, months, years. "Thousands of micro-choices" accumulate until his sensibility has "super-permeated the thing." He is chasing a feeling he calls "undeniability," and hoping the story will "overflow its banks" and show him something he didn't know he believed. That is where writing is learned. It isn't learned in the first draft. Anyone can spill words onto a page, and that part involves little struggle. The skill is built afterward, by sitting with the draft, revising it, then revising it again, slowly forcing the page to bring one's own intuition into focus from its blurry beginnings. That is precisely the part a frontier model now does in seconds. Hand it a rough draft and ask for something better, and it returns a sharpened version. The writer never has to decide which word, or why. Without real wrestling with the machine, it's unclear how much of the writer survives that pass, if any. So concede the point at full strength. For most people who use it, AI will replace most or all of the hard thinking writing normally requires. Most people won't carefully ration it, or write a draft first out of principle. They will use it or they won't. The honest question is whether the benefits might be worth it anyway. They may be. To be sure, the critics have a point that I tend to overlook. I argue that most of us are not training for the writing Olympics (literature was in fact an Olympic medal event from 1912 to 1948), so why not get help? But competent writing isn't the Olympics. It's basic fitness, and you can't hire someone to do your push-ups. Saunders's deeper fear is cultural. He imagines a world "force-fed Muzak all the time, even in concert halls," until "the aesthetic adjusts downward." A flood of machine-averaged prose, he worries, will dull our taste until we can't tell the difference. **That fear underrates readers, and it underrates Saunders.** As long as there are writers like him, the downward drift has a ceiling. People are drawn to work that stands apart, especially work that carries a human being inside it. That pull is close to a law of nature. Saunders himself makes the argument. AI, he says, can't draw on "the experience of having moved through (and heard and smelled and seen and had feelings about) the actual world." You cannot coherently ask whether someone's involvement crossed a line while also insisting that any contact with the tool is anathema. The question is the right one. The verdict skips it. Saunders's revision process clarifies where the threshold sits. What "imprints my sensibility," by his own account, is not the origin of each raw sentence. It is the accumulated pressure of taste: thousands of decisions about sound, rhythm, and what he calls "undeniability," the sense that a reasonable reader is still with him. Those decisions are acts of rejection as much as creation. The pencil kills more than it keeps. A writer who asks a machine for ten versions of a transitional sentence, discards nine, and rewrites the tenth beyond recognition is making exactly that kind of choice. So is a writer who uses a chatbot to argue against her draft, then ignores most of what it says. In both cases the machine is an object of judgment, not its replacement. Whether that's wise in a given instance is a fair question. Nothing Saunders argues shows it is categorically forbidden. His evidence is also thinner than his certainty. His one experiment was to ask AI "to generate some text in my voice," which produced something "so dull and literal" that he lost interest. Asked whether he had tried writing or revising with it, he answered: "I have not. Not for a second." That test proves a machine is a poor counterfeit of George Saunders. Nobody serious was claiming otherwise. It tells us nothing about a writer who never asks the machine to be her, only to give her something to push against. To be sure, Saunders's strongest point deserves full weight. He argues that the messy first draft is where the writer lives. "Your mess has more you in it," he says, seeded with small errors and imprecisions that revision refines into something "utterly you." Editing your own chaos, he says, is "more organic and beneficial" than editing what a machine hands you. He also cites research suggesting that leaning on AI dulls our capabilities, and he is right to worry about a culture that treats ease as the highest good. This is a real cost, and it marks where AI use goes wrong. A writer who never produces her own mess, who starts every piece from the machine's tidy average, is likely to lose exactly what Saunders describes. But look at his language: the mess has *more* you in it. That is a comparison, not a prohibition. It argues for protecting the generative struggle, keeping first drafts human, and staying wary of habit and dependence. It does not support the claim that a single machine-suggested phrase, judged and remade by a working writer, turns her book into someone else's. Writers have always reacted to material they didn't produce: an overheard sentence, a workshop note, a line from another book that lodged in them. Saunders himself says he wants, in fiction, the "subtle record" of every book a writer has read. Influence absorbed and transformed by taste has never been disqualifying. The question was always who was doing the transforming. Collapsing that distinction carries its own danger. Saunders says, "By my definition of writing, writing with AI isn't." If any AI contact disqualifies writing, then a student who uses the tool carefully and one who uses it to fake a Tolstoy essay have committed the same sin. That flattening teaches nothing. Worse, it surrenders the field: if every use is equally damned, there is no reason to learn the difference between using a tool and being used by it. His own standard is far more useful than his anathema, because it tells writers what to protect: a sufficient hand, sustained taste, and choices that make the work "mine." Saunders closes his case by invoking Bartleby: "I [would] prefer not to." That is his right, and for a writer of his gifts it may well be the wise choice. Declining, as he says, is a valid option. But a preference is not a principle, and his principle points somewhere else. Writing worth reading comes from a person whose taste has saturated the work. The enemy was never the machine on the desk. It's the writer who puts down the pencil.
