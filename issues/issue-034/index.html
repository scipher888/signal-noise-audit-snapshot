H. Human/AI essay: fact-check log

H1. The July 2024 outage sentence — SOLID

  • Essay (insertion 2): "We got a preview in July 2024, when one faulty software update, with no hacker and no AI involved, crashed an estimated 8.5 million Windows computers and grounded flights, delayed surgeries and knocked out some 911 systems."
  • Sources and checks:
    • Date and cause. CrowdStrike's technical post [S]: a sensor configuration update was released 19 July 2024 at 04:09 UTC (the evening of 18 July in Pacific time) and "triggered a logic error resulting in a system crash." CrowdStrike's root cause analysis for Channel File 291 [F, full text]: the new template type defined 21 input fields, the sensor code supplied 20, and the mismatch caused an out-of-bounds read and crash. "One faulty software update" is accurate.
    • No hacker. The same post says the issue "is not the result of or related to a cyberattack." The root cause analysis says the bug was reviewed by a third party and is not exploitable by a threat actor. NPR and CNN also report CrowdStrike saying it was not an attack [S].
    • No AI involved. The CrowdStrike analysis says the product has on-sensor AI and machine-learning models, but that the faulty "Rapid Response Content" is "behavioral heuristics, separate and distinct from CrowdStrike's on-sensor AI," written by detection engineers as regular-expression template instances [F]. So the claim holds for the fault. A pedantic reader could note that the vendor's product does use AI, which is a precision point, not an error.
    • 8.5 million. Microsoft's own blog [S]: "We currently estimate that CrowdStrike's update affected 8.5 million Windows devices, or less than one percent of all Windows machines." Reuters, AP and TechCrunch repeat it. The essay says "estimated" and "Windows computers," both fair. It does not say the figure is Microsoft's, and it omits "less than one percent." Mac and Linux machines were not affected (TechCrunch).
    • Grounded flights. The New York Times live coverage [S] says at least five US airlines (Allegiant, American, Delta, Spirit, United) had grounded all flights for a time, per the FAA. CNN [S] reported more than 3,000 US flights cancelled as of Friday evening. Reuters, AP and SEC filings put Delta's recovery at about five days and roughly 7,000 cancellations [S].
    • Delayed surgeries. AP [S]: Harris Health System (Houston) cancelled and rescheduled elective procedures. NBC [S]: Baptist Health in Kentucky cancelled some non-emergency procedures, including a scheduled open-heart surgery. USA Today and CNN also report cancelled medical procedures [S]. "Delayed surgeries" is accurate.
    • 911 systems. USA Today [S]: in Alaska, 911 and non-emergency calls went unanswered at multiple dispatch centers for about seven hours. Coverage also reported disruptions in Virginia, Iowa, New Hampshire, Oregon and Arizona [S]. "Some 911 systems" is the right size.
  • Origin: the figure and the wording come from the comparison drafts, not from Muse. See section J.
  • Severity: PASS. Optional nit: the sentence does not name Microsoft as the source of the 8.5 million estimate.

H2. "We got a preview" and "Power, water, communications, finance and transportation do not fail independently anymore. They fail together" — OVERSTATED

  • Essay: the Muse-base sentence "do not fail independently anymore. They fail together, because they depend on the same internet, the same cloud providers, the same software supply chains," followed by the insertion that calls the July 2024 outage "a preview."
  • Checks: the directional claim (shared dependencies create cross-sector failures) is well supported. CrowdStrike hit airlines, hospitals, 911 centers, banks and broadcasters through one vendor's software. The April 2025 Iberian blackout (H19) took out power, rail, communications and hospital records together. But "do not fail independently anymore" is a flat universal. Most outages are still sector-specific, and the essay's own example did not take down power or water, did not affect Mac or Linux systems, and affected under 1% of Windows machines. Most carriers recovered within days. A bounded, accidental, recoverable outage is a generous "preview" of a deliberate multi-sector cascade (Stage C Kimi 5 raises this).
  • Severity: OVERSTATED (universal and "preview") — non-fail
  • Origin: the "fail together" sentence is Muse's; the "preview" sentence is insertion 2.

H3. CISA: "the federal government's lead civilian cyber-defense agency" — SOLID

  • Essay (insertion 1): "Agencies like the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the federal government's lead civilian cyber-defense agency, exist for a reason."
  • Sources and checks:
    • CISA's own site [S]: "the nation's cyber defense agency" and "National Coordinator for Critical Infrastructure Security and Resilience." A DHS report to Congress (Oct 2024) [S] calls CISA "the operational lead for federal cybersecurity" and "the nation's cyber defense agency." NSM-22 (April 2024) [S] codified the national-coordinator role.
    • "Lead civilian" is the essay's paraphrase, not CISA's own phrase. It is fair, because the military cyber role sits with NSA and Cyber Command, and CISA's own materials use "lead." One qualification: for the response to a specific incident, the FBI leads threat response while CISA leads asset response (Presidential Policy Directive 41 and the National Cyber Incident Response Plan [S]), so "lead" is accurate for defense and coordination, not for every task.
    • The abbreviation is now expanded at first mention, as requested.
  • Context a reader may bring (not an error): reporting in January–February 2026 [S] says CISA fell from about 3,400 staff in January 2025 to under 2,400, and that it has had no Senate-confirmed director since the Plankey nomination stalled. The essay's "exist for a reason" is accurate and, if anything, understates the thinning of the agency. That context would support the essay's argument, so it is not a reason to change the sentence.
  • Severity: PASS

H4. "a person who is homeless, addicted or mentally ill in Los Angeles today can still survive — not comfortably, not justly, but survive" — SOLID (hedged framing; reader flashpoint)

  • Sources: LA County Department of Public Health, press release of 10 March 2026 [F]: 2,208 deaths among people experiencing homelessness in 2024 (300 fewer than in 2023), a mortality rate 4.2 times the county rate, about six deaths a day, and overdose as the leading cause (40% of deaths). LAHSA's 2026 count [S]: an estimated 73,040 people homeless in LA County, 49,002 of them unsheltered.
  • Check: the sentence is hedged ("not comfortably, not justly"), comparative ("in a way that would have been unimaginable for most of human history") and a statement about the typical case. On the numbers, most unhoused people survive a given year (about 3% of the population died in 2024 by the county's rate). But 2,208 deaths and a death rate four times the county's is a lot for "survive." The essay's comparison with prehistory is not something I tested; I did not find a clean like-for-like mortality comparison, so I rate the comparison as framing, not as a fact claim. The rating is SOLID because the factual kernel (people survive within modern systems) holds and the sentence is carefully hedged.
  • Note for the page: this is the line an adversarial reader will quote. A comparison draft (Opus 5.5) advised cutting it as "callous." The author kept it. That is an editorial decision, not a factual defect.
  • Origin: the dump ("homeless, destitute, addicted, and mentally ill people can survive today in and around major cities like LA") is the source. Muse compressed it.
  • Severity: PASS (with the caution above)

H5. "An Elon Musk born 20,000 years ago builds nothing recognizable… dies having sharpened a better stone" — SOLID (rhetorical counterfactual, one nuance)

  • Checks: 20,000 years ago is the Last Glacial Maximum, before agriculture (about 11,000 years ago), before the earliest copper smelting (about 5000 BCE, at Belovode, Serbia) and before writing (Sumer, about 3400 BCE) [S]. The essay's list ("writing, metallurgy, electricity, capital markets and supply chains") is therefore correct as a list of things that did not yet exist.
  • Nuance: "sharpened a better stone" understates what existed. Pottery at Xianrendong Cave in China is dated to about 20,000 years ago (Science, 2012) [S], and Upper Paleolithic people made bone, antler and shell tools and art. People then were not technology-free. This is rhetoric, not a claim about the archaeology, and it makes the point (infrastructure compounds talent) no weaker. "20,000" is Muse's specific figure; the dump says "prehistoric times."
  • Note: the example names a living public figure as a thought experiment. It states nothing about him.
  • Severity: PASS

H6. "Open-weight models ensure those capabilities will not stay locked inside a few responsible labs" and "Foreign adversaries will not pause because we do" — SOLID (direction), with a caveat on "ensure"

  • Sources: the UK AI Security Institute, "How Far Behind the Frontier are Leading Open Weight Models on Cyber?" (17 July 2026) [F]: GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek V4-Pro perform like frontier closed models released 4 to 7 months earlier, a narrower gap than the 6 to 10 months seen in 2025; safeguards on open-weight models "can be removed." Epoch AI, "Open-weight AI models are 6-7 months behind on cyber" (data as of 8 October 2026) [F]: the best open-weight model (GLM-5.3) sits where the closed frontier stood 6 to 7 months earlier. The leading open-weight models are from Chinese developers (Z.ai, DeepSeek, Moonshot).
  • Check: the term "open-weight" is correct for these models (the weights are downloadable; most are not open-source in the full sense). The claim that capabilities will not stay locked in a few labs is supported, with a lag of about half a year. "Ensure" is a strong verb for a lag, and "responsible labs" is a value judgment. Stage C Kimi 11 notes the essay does not mention that closed models are also misused; the record agrees (Anthropic's report on a state-sponsored campaign that used its own model; Google's threat-intelligence reporting on criminal and state actors using Gemini [S][F]). "Foreign adversaries will not pause because we do" is an opinion, but the record on state actors using AI for cyber operations backs its premise (H7).
  • Severity: PASS
  • Note: the essay uses "open-source imitators" in the third paragraph and "open-weight" later (I4).

H7. "Systems that can write code, find vulnerabilities, craft flawless phishing campaigns in any language, and operate persistently without sleep" — OVERSTATED (one word)

  • Sources: Anthropic, "Disrupting the first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign" [S]: a Chinese state-sponsored group used Claude Code to automate roughly 80–90% of a September 2025 campaign against about 30 targets, with reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, exploitation and exfiltration done by the model. Anthropic also said only a small number of intrusions succeeded and that the model sometimes hallucinated credentials or findings. Google Threat Intelligence Group, 11 May 2026 [F]: for the first time, a threat actor using a zero-day exploit "we believe was developed with AI"; state actors sending thousands of repetitive prompts to analyze CVEs; agentic frameworks used for reconnaissance and vulnerability validation; LLMs used to build "higher-fidelity phishing lures."
  • Check: write code, find vulnerabilities and operate persistently are all supported. "In any language" is plausible and not tested. "Flawless" is hyperbole. Google's wording is "higher-fidelity," and Anthropic notes failures and hallucinations. A reader who knows the reports would catch it.
  • Severity: OVERSTATED ("flawless") — non-fail
  • Origin: Muse.

H8. "lowers the skill floor, raises the speed ceiling, and multiplies the number of plausible attackers" — SOLID

  • Sources: Google Threat Intelligence Group (May 2026) [F]: AI tools "lower the barrier for adversaries" and give "expert-level force multipliers"; adversaries use agentic workflows "at machine speed." AISI [F] grades models on tasks from "technical non-expert" up to "expert," and open models are cheap to run (an estimated $0.28 per task for DeepSeek V4-Pro on tasks that Opus 4.5 solved at $12.50).
  • Check: all three clauses are supported in direction. The speed claim rests on vendor and government reporting, not on a controlled comparison.
  • Severity: PASS

H9. "Money is not mostly cash in vaults; it is entries in databases" — SOLID

  • Source: Federal Reserve H.6 release, September 2026 [S]: currency in circulation was about $2.48 trillion in August 2026, which is 12.4% of M1 and 10.6% of M2.
  • Check: most money, on the Fed's measures, is bank deposits and other non-cash balances. Physical currency is about a tenth of M2. ("In vaults" is a figure of speech; most currency is outside banks.)
  • Severity: PASS

H10. "Hospitals do not run on paper charts; they run on networked records, scheduling and billing systems" — SOLID

  • Source: ONC Data Brief 35 [S]: 96% of US non-federal acute-care hospitals had a certified electronic health record system in 2015. The figure is old and the brief says adoption had plateaued. The essay states no number. Ascension's 2024 experience (H18) shows the dependency.
  • Severity: PASS

H11. "automated logistics move food across continents on just-in-time schedules measured in hours" — UNSUPPORTED (as worded)

  • Check: I found no source for "measured in hours." The sources I could reach disagree on how thin the buffers are. A Monash University piece [S] describes just-in-time systems as having minimal buffers. A BBC piece from 2020 [S] argues the chain holds much more inventory than shoppers assume, much of it in warehouses and back rooms. The directional point (modern food distribution is tightly scheduled and computer-coordinated) holds. "Measured in hours" is rhetoric, and it is the least supported number-like claim in the essay.
  • Severity: UNSUPPORTED (as worded) — non-fail
  • Origin: Muse.

H12. Title and "Right now, we have no credible answer" — OVERSTATED

  • Essay: headline "We Have No Plan for the Crash"; "It's what happens the day prevention fails. Right now, we have no credible answer." Against "To be sure, America is not starting from zero. Banks keep backups. Utilities have cybersecurity teams."
  • Sources: Sheltered Harbor, per the ICBA fact sheet and ABA coverage [S]: a financial-sector standard for nightly, encrypted, immutable, isolated backups ("data vaulting") of critical account data, with recovery plans and certification; participants held about 71–72% of US deposit accounts. FS-ISAC's page [F] now says the organization has been wound down and its specification transferred to FS-ISAC for its members. (I did not capture the wind-down date.) The essay's first proposal ("Verified, offline, immutable backups of core financial records, tested… in live restoration exercises") is close to what that standard describes. See also H20 for the national exercises.
  • Check: the claim that there is no plan is stronger than the record. The defensible version is that existing measures are fragmented, voluntary or untested across simultaneous cross-sector loss, which is what the "To be sure" paragraph says. Stage C Kimi 2 raises the internal tension. The essay's thesis does not need "no plan," but it needs "no tested plan."
  • Severity: OVERSTATED — non-fail. The headline claim carries the piece, so this is the finding most worth the author's attention.
  • Origin: the title is Muse's; the dump asks "Is anyone in a position to take action/develop backup plans even asking these questions?", a question the essay turned into an assertion.

H13. "Defenders must be right every time; an attacker only has to be right once" — SOLID (idiom); no source needed, attribution note

  • Check: this is a long-standing security idiom. Its widely cited origin is the IRA statement after the 12 October 1984 Brighton hotel bombing: "Today we were unlucky, but remember we have only to be lucky once, you will have to be lucky always" (Quote Investigator; Wikipedia, "Brighton hotel bombing") [S]. It is not a statistic and cannot be checked. As logic it is looser than it sounds: a layered defense makes an attacker win several times (Stage C Kimi 4, see the bad-premise log).
  • Severity: PASS. The credit to Gemini 4 Argon records where the sentence entered this essay's editing chain, not who invented the idiom.

H14. The two-camp portrait and "A pause, even if America could enforce one — and it cannot globally" — UNSUPPORTED (opinion and characterization)

  • Essay: "Washington, Silicon Valley and the commentariat are locked in a theological war between two camps: pause or accelerate… Both sides talk as if the only decision that matters is how fast we move." Also "Acceleration… rests on a heroic assumption," and the judgment that a pause cannot be enforced globally.
  • Check: these are the author's thesis frame and judgments, not checkable facts. The two-camp picture is a simplification: government and industry work on defender preparation, as the AISI "window for defenders" argument and the exercises in H20 show. "Both sides talk as if…" is a generalization that Stage C Kimi 3 finds too neat. The claim that a pause is not globally enforceable is a reasonable judgment; the essay does not argue it.
  • Severity: UNSUPPORTED (opinion; generalization) — non-fail
  • Origin: the dump's own frame ("Perseverating on pausing vs accelerating AI development misses the more important question").

H15. "Aviation did not become safe… redundant systems, black boxes, emergency procedures, investigations that fed back into design. Nuclear power, finance after 2008, even seatbelts" — SOLID (aviation); the list is unsourced

  • Source: the NTSB [S]: it investigates accidents, determines probable cause and has issued more than 15,800 safety recommendations since 1967; flight recorders have been central to its investigations.
  • Check: the aviation account is a standard and accurate summary. "Nuclear power, finance after 2008, even seatbelts" is a loose list of cases that I did not individually source. They are widely documented (post-accident safety regimes, bank stress testing, seat-belt mandates), but I did not verify any of them in this pass. As analogies, they show a method (assume failure and design for survivability), not a threat model for an adaptive adversary (Stage C Kimi 12).
  • Severity: PASS (aviation); the rest not checked

H16. "A patient intruder can also corrupt the backups months before pulling the trigger" (insertion 4) — SOLID (as a "can" claim)

  • Sources: Veeam's 2024 Ransomware Trends Report [S]: attackers targeted backup repositories in 96% of ransomware attacks and affected them in 76%. Sophos's 2024 report on compromised backups [S]: attackers attempted to compromise backups in 94% of attacks, with 57% success. CISA advisory AA24-038A (7 February 2024) [S]: the PRC-sponsored group Volt Typhoon maintained access in some victim IT environments for at least five years, to pre-position for disruption of critical infrastructure.
  • Check: the sentence says "can," and the record supports that. The statistics show attackers going after backups at the time of the attack, not corrupting them silently for months; I found no documented incident of long-dwell backup corruption, though Volt Typhoon shows multi-year dwell is real. "Months before" is plausible and unsourced as to backups. The Maersk NotPetya recovery (a single surviving domain controller that had been offline during the attack) [S] shows how close a victim can come to having no clean copy, but it was a wiper attack, not months-long poisoning.
  • Severity: PASS
  • Origin: Gemini 4 Argon's draft ("subtly poisoning the data inside 'clean' backups" and "poisoning backup data months in advance"). The essay's sentence is new wording of that idea.

H17. "A pre-agreed baseline date from which the economy can restart if the live records are lost" (insertion 5) — PROPOSAL, not a factual claim

  • Check: this is a policy proposal. I found no existing US framework that uses a pre-agreed economy-wide restart date. The nearest real mechanism is data vaulting that preserves end-of-day account balances (H12), which would give a recoverable snapshot date but not an agreed rollback rule. Stage C Kimi 8 asks who picks the date and what happens to transactions after it. The sentence does not claim the mechanism exists.
  • Severity: n/a (proposal). It is the least supported of the five insertions and the one Stage C presses hardest.
  • Origin: Gemini 4 Argon's draft ("the government must be able to declare a baseline date—say, the first of the previous month—and reboot economic life from an incorruptible physical record"). The essay drops the example date and the optical-media detail.

H18. "Paper and local-cache protocols in hospitals that allow care to continue for weeks, not hours, without central systems" — SOLID (feasible, degraded)

  • Sources: NPR and Becker's [S]: Ascension's hospitals lost their EHR, patient portal, phones and ordering systems to ransomware on 8 May 2024, used paper charts, faxes and manual medication processes, and had the EHR restored systemwide on 14 June 2024. NPR's headline reports staffers describing "harrowing lapses."
  • Check: weeks of care on paper happened, which answers Stage C Opus 4 (that it is infeasible; see the bad-premise log). It also shows the cost: delayed results and medication-safety risk. The essay asks for protocols that allow care to continue, which is a fair ask, and says nothing about how well. A reader could ask for the word "degraded."
  • Severity: PASS

H19. "past blackouts were local, temporary and analog-supported in ways today's systems no longer are" — OVERSTATED

  • Source: the ENTSO-E final report on the 28 April 2025 Iberian blackout [S]: it began at 12:33 CEST, Portugal's transmission system was restored in about 12 hours and Spain's in about 16, and about 60 million people were affected. A peer-reviewed analysis of the Portuguese health service [S] and other reporting describe hospitals on limited generators, lost access to digital records, failed emergency communications, stopped rail and failed traffic lights.
  • Check: "temporary" is right. "Local" is not right for a blackout that covered two countries and about 60 million people. "Analog-supported" is arguable, since the 2025 event already hit digital dependencies. The event supports the essay's thesis better than the sentence does. The essay's following sentence ("We survived because the surrounding system survived") holds.
  • Severity: OVERSTATED (the word "local") — non-fail
  • Origin: Muse.

Bad-premise and misread log

These critiques rest on a premise the essay or the record does not support. They are logged, not adopted.

  1. Opus #4, "hospitals cannot function for weeks on paper … technically infeasible" (self-rated FLOOR). Wrong premise. The essay asks for protocols that allow care to continue, and the record shows a major system doing it: Ascension's hospitals ran on paper charts, faxes and manual medication processes from the 8 May 2024 ransomware attack until the EHR was restored systemwide on 14 June 2024 (about five weeks). NPR reported staffers describing "harrowing lapses" in that period. So the claim that it is impossible fails. The kernel that survives is that it is degraded and error-prone, which is Kimi 9 (rated SCOPE). Not a FLOOR.
  2. Opus #6, "pause can't be coordinated globally, so American-only resilience is contradictory." Wrong premise. A pause only works if rivals also comply. A bank's offline backups, a utility's segmented controls and a hospital's paper protocols work without anyone's cooperation. That asymmetry is the argument for resilience. A small kernel survives: shared cloud and software supply chains mean "domestic" is never fully domestic. The essay itself says the sectors "depend on the same internet, the same cloud providers."
  3. Opus #3, first half: "the essay argues we're wrongly focused on prevention." Misread. The essay says "Prevention is essential" and "Keep competing … keep working on alignment and defense." Its target is prevention alone. The second half (finite budgets; no priority ranking) lands and converges with Kimi 10 and Opus 5.
  4. Opus #1, "the central premise depends on AI being categorically different." Partly misread. The essay says outright that "AI does not invent this vulnerability" and rests its case on changed economics (skill floor, speed, number of attackers) and on the failure of prevention in general. A crash plan is justified by consequences, not by AI being new. The kernel (the essay never says why existing cyber-defence is not just scaled up) overlaps with Kimi 1 and 6.
  5. Opus #2, "the essay never defines the crash." Partly misread. The opening scenario and the sentence "The risk AI raises is simultaneity" do define it: simultaneous loss of finance, communications, logistics and utilities. What is missing is a threshold at which the plan activates. That is a SCOPE point, not a missing definition.
  6. Kimi #4, "'defenders must be right every time' cuts against the resilience thesis." Mostly misread. In the essay the sentence attacks the strategy of staying one step ahead forever, which is the prevention-only position. It supports the resilience argument. The kernel that lands is narrower: in a layered defence an attacker has to win several times, so the maxim is looser as logic than as rhetoric. It is also an old security idiom (see audit section H13). HYGIENE, not ARGUMENT.
  7. Kimi #5, CrowdStrike "proves only dependence, not AI catastrophe." Overreads. The essay says in the sentence itself that the incident had "no hacker and no AI involved" and calls it a "preview" of dependence.
