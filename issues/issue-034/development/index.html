A. Machine essay: fact-check log

A1. Molina and Rowland, CFCs, and "within about fifteen years … the Montreal Protocol"

  • Essay: "In the 1970s, scientists Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland proposed that chlorofluorocarbons … were destroying the ozone layer. … Yet within about fifteen years, through open research, public debate, and political pressure, the world had signed the Montreal Protocol."
  • Sources: UNEP, Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion: 2022, which gives the 1974 Nature paper and the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Molina, Rowland and Crutzen; NOAA's Twenty Questions (Vienna Convention 1985, Montreal Protocol 1987); the UN Treaty Series (Montreal Protocol concluded 16 September 1987).
  • Checks: 1974 to the 1987 signing is 13 years. The protocol entered into force on 1 January 1989, which is 15 years. "About fifteen" is correct on one reading and slightly long on the other. The 1970s dating and the attribution are right.
  • Severity: PASS

A2. "the ozone layer is now on a path to recovery"

  • Source: UNEP 2022 assessment: total column ozone is expected to return to 1980 values around 2066 in the Antarctic, around 2045 in the Arctic, and around 2040 for the near-global average. The Antarctic is already recovering despite large year-to-year variability. Outside the Antarctic, the evidence of recovery since 1996 has "low confidence."
  • Check: "on a path to recovery" is a careful phrase and is accurate.
  • Severity: PASS

A3. "Their claim was inconvenient to a large industry and initially contested" and the causal story

  • Check: "initially contested" is correct. The essay credits "open research, public debate, and political pressure." The record adds two accelerants the essay omits: the 1985 Antarctic ozone-hole discovery and, after NASA's Ozone Trends Panel, DuPont's March 1988 reversal on a CFC phase-out. The essay also leaves out that drop-in substitutes (HCFCs, then HFCs) made the phase-out cheaper. These omissions flatter the "error correction" reading a little. Stage C picks up the easy-case point (Kimi 1, Opus 1).
  • Severity: PRECISION (compression) — non-fail

A4. Lysenkoism "that the state elevated above genetics for roughly three decades"

  • Sources: the Genetics review "The pushback against state interference in science" (PMC8664584) says Lysenkoism "was most active since the mid-1930s till the mid-1960s." The 2019 Genetics article on the August 1948 session says genetics returned to educational programs after Khrushchev's removal in 1964. Isis dates the formal "proscription of genetics" to 1948–1965.
  • Check: "roughly three decades" fits the mid-1930s to 1964. Counting from the 1948 session, which made it formal, it was about 16 years. Both readings are defensible and the essay does not say which it means.
  • Severity: PRECISION — non-fail

A5. "Scientists who objected lost their posts, and some lost their lives"

  • Sources: Nature, "Stalin's war on genetic science": Vavilov was arrested on 6 August 1940, sentenced to death in July 1941, had the sentence commuted, and died of starvation in a Saratov prison on 26 January 1943. Genetics 2019: "many were shot during the nation-wide Great Purge from 1936 to 1938, although the exact numbers … are unavailable." Vavilov was dismissed in 1935.
  • Check: correct. One nuance: the shootings sit in the Great Purge, which is wider than Lysenko alone. The essay's phrasing is a fair compression.
  • Severity: PASS

A6. Great Leap Forward: "local officials reported fantastical harvests because reporting the truth was dangerous. Grain was requisitioned on the basis of numbers everyone knew were false"

  • Sources: Li and Yang, "The Causes of China's Great Leap Famine, 1959–1961" (Economic Development and Cultural Change, 2005, as surfaced in search) on inflated output reports driving compulsory procurement. Sen, "Democracy as a Universal Value" (Journal of Democracy, 1999): faulty policies "remained uncorrected for three full years" because there was no free press or opposition.
  • Check: the mechanism (falsified reports, procurement set on the inflated figures, danger in reporting truthfully) is well documented. "Everyone knew" overstates it; see A8.
  • Severity: PASS

A7. "tens of millions of people starved"

  • Sources: search summaries of the demographic literature give about 16.5 million (early Chinese estimates) up to Dikötter's 45 million and Yang Jisheng's 36 million; Sen writes "nearly 30 million."
  • Check: at the centre of the range, "tens of millions" is accurate. At the low end it would be "more than ten million." "Starved" is also the common shorthand for the famine's excess deaths.
  • Severity: PRECISION — non-fail

A8. "Numbers everyone knew were false" and "These were not failures of soil or weather"

  • Check: "everyone knew" is overstated. Local officials knew, but some senior leaders were deceived by the figures. The flat denial of weather is stronger than the literature supports. Sen himself notes that commentators "settle for the simplicity" of pointing to floods and droughts, while the mainstream view puts the policy at the centre. Weather is usually given some secondary role. I did not open a weather-attribution study, so this is marked as unverified on that point.
  • Severity: FACT-SOFT — non-fail

A9. Sen: "no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a relatively free press"

  • Sources: Sen, "Democracy as a Universal Value," Journal of Democracy (1999): "in the terrible history of famines in the world, no substantial famine has ever occurred in any independent and democratic country with a relatively free press." Sen, Development as Freedom (1999): "no famine has ever taken place in the history of the world in a functioning democracy." NYT, Bernstein review (1999), and NYT, "Does Democracy Avert Famine?" (2003).
  • Checks:
    • The essay's wording is a hybrid of Sen's two formulations: "substantial" and "relatively free press" come from the first, "functioning democracy" from the second. It is a paraphrase (no quotation marks), so there is no QUOTE mismatch.
    • It drops Sen's "independent." That word does real work: Sen explicitly places the Irish famine and British India under "alien rule," so they are not counterexamples. Without it, a reader can cite Ireland (Stage C Opus 2 did).
    • The essay's gloss on his argument ("when bad news can be published and leaders can be removed, those in power have strong reasons to respond") matches Sen: democratic governments "have to win elections and face public criticism."
    • The claim is contested in the literature. Critics cite food crises in democratic settings and argue that elections and a free press do not always lead to action. The essay presents it as settled, with "memorably" doing some rhetorical lifting.
  • Severity: PRECISION (omits "independent") — non-fail

A10. Ogallala: "took thousands of years to fill and is being pumped far faster than it recharges"

  • Sources: Kansas Geological Survey, The High Plains Aquifer: much of the water accumulated over thousands to tens of thousands of years, including during the Pleistocene ice ages. USGS (Professional Paper 1400-B; HA 730-D): recharge ranges from 0.024 inch per year (parts of Texas) to 6 inches per year (south-central Kansas); in low-recharge areas pumping "could be more than 100 times the recharge." A 2014 Earth's Future paper says total irrigation pumping exceeds average recharge by a factor of 2–7. USGS also notes water-level rises in parts of southern Nebraska.
  • Checks: "thousands of years to fill" is fair. "Pumped far faster than it recharges" is right for the southern and central High Plains and in aggregate. The aquifer is not uniformly overdrawn, though, and the essay treats it as one body. Wording note: the aquifer is the High Plains aquifer, of which the Ogallala Formation is the principal unit.
  • Severity: FACT-SOFT (uniform overdraft) — non-fail
  • Origin: this sentence comes from the run-A paragraph, which the merge condensed.

A11. "A century ago, almost no one was worried about atmospheric carbon, antibiotic resistance, or the fragility of global supply chains"

  • Sources: Arrhenius (1896) estimated that doubling atmospheric CO2 would raise temperature by about 5–6 °C; Callendar (1938) argued fossil-fuel CO2 was warming the planet; Fleming's 1945 Nobel lecture warned that underdosing penicillin could select for resistance.
  • Check: a handful of scientists (Arrhenius, then Callendar) did raise carbon, so "almost no one" is the correct hedge. Penicillin did not exist in 1926 (discovered 1928), and global supply chains in today's sense did not either. The sentence is accurate.
  • Severity: PASS

A12. "local newspapers disappear and no one replaces them"

  • Source: Medill, State of Local News 2025: nearly 3,500 US newspapers have closed since 2005, 213 counties have no local news source, and about 50 million Americans have limited or no access to local news.
  • Check: supported.
  • Severity: PASS

A13. "platforms reward outrage over accuracy"

  • Sources: Brady et al. (Science Advances, 2021): likes and shares reinforce future moral-outrage posts. Milli et al. (PNAS Nexus, 2025): engagement-based ranking amplified angry, partisan and out-group-hostile content relative to chronological feeds.
  • Check: the outrage half is supported. "Over accuracy" is not directly established by those studies; they do not show that platforms systematically prefer inaccurate content. The essay offers it as a one-line opinion.
  • Severity: FACT-SOFT — non-fail

A14. Universal and characterizing claims

  • Essay: "Every regime that destroyed its own feedback loops did so in the name of something its supporters believed was urgent and good. The Lysenkoists believed they were feeding the people."
  • Check: "every" is a sweeping universal that cannot be checked in full. For Lysenko, the Genetics review does cite the hope of ending food shortages with "miracle-working" methods (and, alongside it, ideology, careerism and Stalin's attempt to subordinate science). "Believed" is a claim about motives. It is a fair gloss for the example and an uncheckable generalization.
  • Severity: FACT-SOFT (rhetorical universal) — non-fail

A15. "A species driven to extinction does not return when we regret it"

  • Check: true as stated. Recent de-extinction efforts produce engineered proxies, not the original species. I did not source this pass.
  • Severity: PASS

C3. Overlap with the author's raw brain dump

The model never saw the brain dump (human_drafts_shown_to_model: false in the transcript). Compared honestly:

Real overlap

  1. Civilization as the unit that matters. The brain dump opens on civilization as the thing everything depends on. The essay frames the stakes in terms of "a civilization" that keeps or loses its ability to detect and fix mistakes. Counts of the word are 7 (draft) and 3 (essay), so this is a shared frame, not heavy phrasing.
  2. Resilience and recovery. The brain dump's core concern is what happens when something critical breaks and what the backup plan is. The essay's core claim is that a society can "recover from almost anything" if it can notice and correct. Both are about surviving failure, not preventing every failure. The essay's own exception, "sudden annihilation," is close to the draft's worry (a sudden, total loss of digital infrastructure).
  3. Move one level up. The draft says that arguing "pause vs. accelerate" misses the more important question. The essay says to look "one level deeper, beneath the resources themselves." Both reframe from the first-order debate to the meta-level.
  4. Both describe damage that cannot be undone: the essay's irreversibility paragraph and the draft's wiped-out records.

Differences

  • The essay is about the information and institutional layer (press, science, courts, elections, humility). The draft is about the physical and digital infrastructure layer (banking records, power grid, hospitals, supply chain) and about backups, redundancy and fallbacks.
  • The essay never mentions backups, crashes, redundancy, infrastructure or the pause-vs-accelerate debate. It mentions AI once, in passing. The draft is AI-centred.
  • The essay's proposed action (defend channels that carry bad news) is not the draft's (build backup plans for critical digital infrastructure).
  • The essay's history (ozone, Lysenko, the Great Leap, Sen) and its Sen/Montreal evidence have no counterpart in the draft.

Caveats to disclose

  • The questions have a shared upstream. The two questions that seeded the machine essay were written by the author, who was working on the brain dump's subject at the same time. The machine saw the questions, not the draft, but the questions and the draft share an author and a mood. Some overlap on "what protects long-term quality of life" and "what do we do about it" is therefore expected.
  • Selection effect, by Grok Bot. Grok Bot compared four model essays and chose between them after having seen the brain dump. Its recommendation noted that the Opus thesis "is also adjacent to your piece's resilience theme." That adjacency was a (secondary) reason for the pick, so the final essay was chosen in part because it sat near the author's draft. The primary reasons were that Opus was the strongest argument, and that its two runs converged. The audit page should say so rather than present the overlap as pure coincidence.
  • Not a fresh test. Opus 5.5 already tends to land on "institutions and feedback" answers when asked about long-term flourishing, as shown by the two-run convergence. The brain dump's overlap is therefore weaker evidence of anything than the convergence between the runs.
