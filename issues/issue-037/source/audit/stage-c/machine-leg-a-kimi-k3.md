# Stage C — machine-leg-a-kimi-k3

## Objections

1. **Quote/paraphrase:** “I can’t independently confirm the flight” vs. later “that obstacle has now been removed at least once.”  
   **Objection:** The essay brackets its central factual premise as unverified, then later speaks as though the reuse barrier has been definitively breached. The caveat is not carried through the argument.  
   **Why it lands:** A careful reader sees the whole ranking and forecast depend on an event the author explicitly says he cannot confirm, yet the conclusion uses confirmatory language.  
   **Severity:** FLOOR

2. **Quote/paraphrase:** “With 2026 computers, materials and factories” older feats become routine; “what stays hard is whatever is still hard today.”  
   **Objection:** The ranking rule is circular and presentist: past achievements are discounted because their problems were later solved, while current achievements rank high precisely because they are unsolved. It measures closeness to the present frontier, not difficulty across history.  
   **Why it lands:** The essay claims a transhistorical standard, but the standard collapses into “hard now means hard.”  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

3. **Quote/paraphrase:** “Starship’s catch comes sixth” compared against Apollo, Webb, LHC, LIGO, ISS.  
   **Objection:** This compares a single maneuver/event with entire programs and institutions. If “the catch” is ranked, it is too small to sit beside Apollo; if the whole Starship program is ranked, the essay has not actually assessed engines, factories, refurbishment, flight rate, or payload economics.  
   **Why it lands:** The list keeps sliding between event and program whenever convenient.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

4. **Quote/paraphrase:** Ahead of ISS “which a ship this size would have made far simpler to build.”  
   **Objection:** This uses a hypothetical future vehicle to downgrade a past achievement. By that move, almost any historical project can be demoted by imagining later tools; but the essay elsewhere denies that move when it wants to keep Apollo or Webb high.  
   **Why it lands:** The counterfactual is applied selectively.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

5. **Quote/paraphrase:** “A single catch is not yet reuse,” but the catch still “shows the physics works” and removes the sixty-year obstacle.  
   **Objection:** The essay wants the demotion for not demonstrating reuse and the historical significance of having demonstrated reuse. One caught ship does not establish thermal protection durability, engine life, rapid turnaround, payload mass fraction, or economics.  
   **Why it lands:** The text itself names the missing evidence, then proceeds as if the evidence were sufficient.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

6. **Quote/paraphrase:** “Its builders could learn by destroying uncrewed prototypes… a freedom Apollo and Webb never had.”  
   **Objection:** The metric is inconsistently punitive. Iterative destructive testing is treated as lowering difficulty for Starship, while the essay does not similarly discount entries that were ground-testable, repairable, iterative, or benefited from precursor programs. It also treats exploding prototypes as evidence of ease rather than evidence of operating near failure.  
   **Why it lands:** A reader can see the same fact being spun downward for Starship but not upward for rivals.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

7. **Quote/paraphrase:** “In ten years… most of the roughly two billion people still offline come within reach of a signal.”  
   **Objection:** “Within reach of a signal” is not “online.” The sentence slides past affordability, devices, power, backhaul, literacy, regulation, censorship, and demand.  
   **Why it lands:** The essay chooses a coverage metric that sounds like a social result.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

8. **Quote/paraphrase:** “Space telescopes get cheaper because they no longer need to fold up as Webb did and can be built heavy and simple.”  
   **Objection:** This reduces Webb’s cost to folding and mass. The essay’s own difficulty criteria—one-shot deployment, alignment, thermal stability, verification, no repair crew—would remain major cost drivers even with cheaper launch.  
   **Why it lands:** It contradicts the essay’s earlier account of why Webb was hard.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

9. **Quote/paraphrase:** Aviation precedent: “a century after the Wright brothers…” while also “It never becomes bus fare.”  
   **Objection:** The airline analogy is asked to support mass off-Earth settlement even though the essay concedes spaceflight remains energetically and economically unlike aviation. A century of aviation scaled because destinations had air, water, food, weather tolerance, and short routes; orbit and Mars do not.  
   **Why it lands:** The analogy is invoked for optimism and then qualified into near-uselessness.  
   **Severity:** ARGUMENT

10. **Quote/paraphrase:** “Moving there is… as emigrating to Australia was in 1850.”  
    **Objection:** The analogy domesticates an environment with no breathable air, no ecology, radiation exposure, closed-loop life support, and total dependence on high-technology supply chains. “Hard but real” is doing too much rhetorical work.  
    **Why it lands:** A careful reader notices the comparison makes an unprecedented dependence sound like a difficult sea voyage.  
    **Severity:** ARGUMENT

11. **Quote/paraphrase:** Dense exact figures: “50,000 droplets,” “344” single-failure points, “one ten-thousandth the width of a proton,” “more than twice the thrust,” “$50,000 per kilogram.”  
    **Objection:** The essay piles up brittle precision without sourcing or uncertainty ranges, immediately after admitting it cannot verify the central event. The rhetorical effect is authority; the epistemic footing is thin.  
    **Why it lands:** The more exact the numbers sound, the more the opening caveat damages trust.  
    **Severity:** HYGIENE

12. **Quote/paraphrase:** The hundred-year “hopeful picture”: hundreds of thousands off Earth, one settlement nearly self-sufficient, “Earth is cleaner for it.”  
    **Objection:** The end state requires technologies and institutions the essay has not argued for: closed-loop life support, in-space manufacturing of critical spares, governance, radiation medicine, debris control, and energy balance. Listing “crowded orbits, unwritten rules and low gravity” afterward does not integrate those risks into the timeline.  
    **Why it lands:** The forecast is labeled optimistic but still narrated as a coherent path rather than a branching set of failures.  
    **Severity:** SCOPE

**Objection count: 12**
