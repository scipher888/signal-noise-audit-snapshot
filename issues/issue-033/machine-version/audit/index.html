B. Machine essay

B1. Robin Sloan, “home-cooked”

  • Essay: “The writer Robin Sloan once described an app he made for his family as ‘home-cooked’ software, built for an audience of four, with no ambition to scale.”
  • Source: Robin Sloan, “An app can be a home-cooked meal” (robinsloan.com, February 2020; fetched).
    • It is about BoopSnoop, a messaging app "for, and with, my family," downloaded by "four people" and holding at "four daily active users."
    • Sloan: "I am the programming equivalent of a home cook." "No one else will ever use it." He contrasts it with being "professional and scalable."
    • Updates through February 2026 say the family still uses it.
  • Checks:
    • The attribution, the family app and the audience of four are correct.
    • "No ambition to scale" is a fair paraphrase.
    • The quoted word "home-cooked" appears in his title ("home-cooked meal"), but he does not use the phrase "home-cooked software." The quotation marks wrap only "home-cooked," which is defensible.
    • Note: Sloan says he built it with ordinary code, not AI. The essay doesn't claim otherwise.
  • Severity: QUOTE-MINOR / PRECISION — non-fail
  • Disposition: leave

B2. Desktop publishing in the 1980s

  • Essay: pundits predicted the end of graphic design; menus and posters got better; designers moved toward brand and identity work; a few real publishing businesses appeared.
  • Check: the dates are correct: Apple Macintosh (1984), Aldus PageMaker (1985) and the LaserWriter (1985), with Paul Brainerd credited with coining "desktop publishing." Contemporary worry that DTP would deskill typesetting and design is well documented, and typesetting as a trade did largely collapse. "Pundits predicted the end of graphic design" is a fair generalization, but no specific pundit is named. The claim that designers moved toward brand and identity work is a reasonable historical gloss, not a quantified one.
  • Note: the essay omits that typesetters really were displaced, which slightly flatters the "nobody lost" reading.
  • Severity: PRECISION — non-fail
  • Disposition: leave

B3. The spreadsheet “did not abolish accounting”

  • Check: VisiCalc (1979), Lotus 1-2-3 (1983) and Excel followed. The "amateur modelers / what-if" description is standard. Accounting employment grew after spreadsheets, while bookkeeping and accounting-clerk roles shrank. NPR's Planet Money "Spreadsheets!" episode is the usual popular source for this. The direction is correct.
  • Severity: PASS / PRECISION (clerks omitted)

B4. “some of the most consequential errors in modern finance and research have lived in cells nobody double-checked”

  • Check: well supported, though the essay names no examples:
    • Reinhart–Rogoff "Growth in a Time of Debt" (2010), whose Excel range error was exposed by Herndon, Ash and Pollin in 2013.
    • JPMorgan's 2012 "London Whale" loss, where the JPM Task Force report cites a spreadsheet VaR-model error (dividing by a sum instead of an average).
    • Gene-name autocorrection corrupting genomics papers (Ziemann et al., 2016).
  • Severity: PASS (unnamed but true)

B5. “Ten years ago ‘proficient in Excel’ was a line on a résumé; today it is assumed, like literacy.”

  • Check: overstated. "Proficient in Excel" still appears in many job listings today. This is rhetoric, not fact.
  • Severity: PRECISION — non-fail

Dual audit section C

C. Convergence: machine essay vs. the unaided human draft (disclose on the audit page)

The machine saw only the seed question. It did not see the unaided draft or the author piece; the transcript records human_drafts_shown_to_model: false. Even so, it independently reached several of the author's themes:

  1. Building for yourself: small tools "for themselves and the people around them," as the "dominant mode" (Sloan's "home-cooked" app). The draft says "building stuff for yourself / asking AI to build whatever you want."
  2. Building ability loses its market premium: "whatever is easy to acquire becomes a baseline expectation." This is close to the draft's monetization-becomes-difficult idea. The machine frames it as the skill losing its premium; it never makes the draft's "why buy what your AI can copy?" argument.
  3. Human relationships stay scarce: "Distribution, relationships, and credibility… trust… getting other humans to adopt something." This is adjacent to the draft's "human contact" refuge.
  4. Judgment and taste as the bottleneck: AI is weaker at "what should exist, for whom, and why?" This loosely parallels the draft's "unverifiable fields." The machine never mentions philosophy or ethics. The author freeze's closing line, "What should exist, and is it any good?", is strikingly close to the machine's question, and the two were produced independently. Opus 5.5 is the base model in both lines, so this may be a model habit rather than coincidence. Disclose it.
  5. Satisfaction and agency: the machine's "broad, quiet expansion of agency" echoes the draft's "immensely satisfying on a personal level."

Not in the human draft: the desktop-publishing and spreadsheet history, maintenance and "amateur bugs," the vending-machine vs. tutor divide, and the "hyphenate" argument.

Shared framing: both freezes set up "gold rush vs. eulogy" and "fortune vs. obsolescence" style dichotomies. The author freeze reaches a narrower, more pessimistic economic verdict ("Far less of it will be sold"). The machine reaches a broader, gentler one ("not a fortune and not obsolescence").

Machine: objections that land

  1. Maintenance cuts against its own optimism (Opus 3, Kimi 9). The "shadow side" is treated as a manageable footnote, and "does not depend on market conditions" is in tension with services breaking. LANDS.
  2. Distribution claims ("most," "dominant mode," "millions") rest on illustrative examples (Kimi 3, Opus 2). Plus: why didn't end-user programming (no-code, macros) already deliver this? LANDS.
  3. Historical analogies may not transfer (Kimi 1, Opus 1). DTP and spreadsheets automated bounded tasks; the essay also omits the typesetters and clerks who were displaced. LANDS (see dual audit B2/B3).
  4. The bottleneck shift can concentrate rather than spread opportunity (Kimi 7, Opus 4). "Not obsolescence" is compatible with greater inequality. LANDS.
  5. "Mostly curiosity rather than talent" (Kimi 8, Opus 5). This ignores access, time and employer permission. LANDS.
  6. High-stakes amateur tools (nurse handoff, tenants) cross the line the "home-cooked" analogy covers (Kimi 5). LANDS.
  7. "Excel is assumed" overgeneralizes (Kimi 4); hyphenate vs. institutional control (Kimi 6); undefined cohort (Kimi 10); agency without evidence (Opus 6). Minor.
