They are interesting, but they are a second essay. Here they serve as the answer to "so what's left?" **What I cut:** The throat-clearing about time constraints and the X-post bot as a standalone item. I repurposed the X-post bot as evidence that human judgment stays in the loop. --- # Step 2: Pressure-Test Your draft has a strong instinct and several soft spots. Here is what a hostile reader will say. **1. "Code was never the moat."** This is the strongest objection. Enterprises pay for Salesforce or Workday for accountability, compliance, security, uptime, and someone to call (and blame). Network products like Uber, Slack, and LinkedIn sell their user base, not their features. You can't prompt your way to a million drivers. *Fortification:* Narrow the claim. The thesis applies to the vast middle of single-purpose utilities and personal-productivity SaaS, not to network or trust businesses. That is still an enormous market. **2. "You're an enthusiast. Most people don't want to build anything."** Your evidence comes from you and your son, a classic selection-bias problem. People pay for convenience they could replicate, like Netflix over piracy or meal kits over recipes. *Fortification:* "Building" is turning into "asking." The barrier isn't skill anymore. It's knowing you can ask. That gap closes fast. **3. "The money isn't disappearing. It's moving to Grok, Apple, and Spotify."** Your own draft undermines "monetization becomes impossible." Every tool you describe runs on someone's paid platform. *Fortification:* Embrace this as the sharper version of the thesis. Monetization doesn't die. It migrates up the stack and squeezes out the middle. **4. Jevons paradox.** Cheaper production usually means more consumption. Desktop publishing didn't kill designers, and spreadsheets didn't kill accountants. *Fortification:* Concede it cleanly. More software will exist than ever. Less of it will be *sold*. **5. "Seven months and nothing to sell" reads as personal failure, not structural insight.** *Fortification:* Own it as the hook. The point is that the products work but have no market, and that's the signal. **6. The philosophy argument eats itself.** You argue AI is weak where outputs can't be verified, then admit that without verification humans can't claim to judge either. *Fortification:* Resolve it. Unverifiable judgment stays human because of accountability and legitimacy, not accuracy. Someone has to own the call. **7. "Why share instead of sell?" is underdeveloped.** Bespoke tools are, by definition, built for one person. Why would anyone want yours? *Fortification:* What gets shared is the *idea*: the spec, the workflow, the prompt. The description has become the blueprint, which is exactly why it can't be sold. --- # Step 3: Op-Ed Structure - **Lede:** Confession. Seven months of building, nothing to sell, and that's the forecast, not the failure. - **Nut graph:** Software is turning from product into recipe. The app economy assumes building is hard, and that assumption is collapsing. - **Evidence:** 1. Your son's college app replaces half a dozen paid products. 2. The description is the blueprint. Features can't be defended when describing them is enough to clone them. 3. The question shifts from "Can you build it?" to "What should exist, and is it any good?" --- # Step 4: The Draft ## Nobody Will Buy Your App *When AI lets anyone conjure software on demand, the money moves, and it isn't moving to the people who make apps.* Seven months ago, I started building software with AI. I'm not a professional developer, and I'm well past the age when anyone expects me to become one. Today, I have nothing to sell: no startup, no product, no paying customers. I've come to believe that isn't a failure. It's a forecast. What I do have is a phone full of tools built for an audience of one. One is an executive-function app that reads emails forwarded from a handful of domains I care about. It proposes calendar entries, ranks everything else into a high-medium-low "talk list," and, when I'm ready to act, nags me every 30 minutes until the task is done. Another is a daily "state of AI" micro-podcast that an AI agent writes, produces, and uploads to Spotify so it's waiting for me on my commute. A third agent watches airfares for an upcoming family trip and tells me when to buy. None of it is marketable. That's the point. When AI drives the cost of building software toward zero, software stops being a product and becomes something closer to a recipe. You don't buy it. You describe it, and it appears. The entire app economy, from app stores to subscriptions to the long tail of software-as-a-service, rests on one assumption: that building is hard. That assumption is collapsing faster than the industry admits. The first casualties will be the companies selling exactly the kind of software amateurs like me now make for ourselves over a weekend. Consider my son, a college student. Using an AI agent, he built what I can only describe as a personal academic operating system. It pulls from his school email and class websites. It tracks every assignment, syllabus, quiz, and exam, along with whatever outside activities he adds. It turns recorded lectures into complete study guides and adds subtitles to video lectures. It then converts an entire course's material into interactive practice quizzes that resurface the questions he misses at intervals designed for retention. Classmates can log in and compete for the high score. Count the businesses inside that one project: an assignment tracker, a calendar and planner, a transcription service, a flashcard platform, a spaced-repetition app, a study-game company. Each of those categories supports real firms with real subscription revenue. My son didn't compare pricing tiers. He described what he wanted, and then he kept describing until it was right. This exposes the deeper problem for anyone selling software: **the description is now the blueprint.** If I tried to market my executive-function app, my pitch would explain what it does, and that explanation is all a competent AI needs to build it for you. Features used to be defensible because implementing them took teams and months. Now implementing them takes a paragraph. So the rational move isn't to sell. It's to share the idea, the workflow, the prompt, and let others build their own version. Build and share, not build and monetize. There's a second reason bespoke wins. Off-the-shelf software is a compromise designed for an imaginary median user. My podcast covers exactly what I care about, and I retune it whenever my interests shift. No subscription service could profitably offer that level of fit to one listener. When customization costs nothing, generic products lose their only advantage, which was being cheaper than custom. To be sure, code was never the whole moat. Corporations buy enterprise software for accountability, compliance, and someone to call (and blame) when it breaks. Uber and LinkedIn sell networks, not features, and you can't prompt your way to a million drivers. Most people have no desire to build anything. And notice whose names keep appearing in my own story: Grok, Spotify, Apple. The money isn't vanishing. It's migrating up the stack to the platforms that do the conjuring. But none of that rescues the app maker in the middle. Trust businesses and network businesses will survive, and the platforms will thrive. The vast middle of single-purpose utilities and productivity subscriptions will not. "Most people won't build" assumes building still looks like building. Increasingly it looks like asking, and soon AI agents will propose the tool before you think to ask. We'll see more software created than ever before. Far less of it will be sold. So what stays scarce? Two things. The first is human presence: work where another person on the other side *is* the product, like care, teaching, counseling, or the deal closed over dinner. The second is judgment in domains without answer keys. I've been entering an AI philosophy competition, mostly to watch how my own thinking compares with a machine's. It's humbling, and revealing. AI dazzles wherever answers can be checked: code that runs, math that resolves, facts that verify. Where there's no objective test, it flounders. Even in my small experiments, my X-posting bot does better when I bring the thought and let it help me refine, rather than handing it the whole job. One might object that if there's no objective standard, humans can't credibly judge either. Fair, but that's exactly why such judgment stays human. In ethics, politics, and culture, we don't hand decisions to people because they're provably right. We hand them over because someone must own the call and answer for it. Legitimacy, not accuracy, is the scarce resource. For forty years, the defining question in technology was "Can you build it?" Then it became "Can you sell it?" The next question is harder: "What should exist, and is it any good?" The machines will build whatever we ask. Deciding what to ask, and living with the answer, stays with us. So build for yourself, and share what's interesting. And if you want to get paid, find the work where there's a person on the other side. --- **Word count:** ~1,080 **Editor's notes before you file:** - **Name the competition.** Link it or name it in the piece. The relevant comparison is the total effort of owning a homemade tool against the price of a dependable service. Your argument is strongest for personal tools whose functions are relatively straightforward to reproduce. - **Being able to build does not mean wanting to build.** People routinely pay for things they could make themselves. Your enthusiasm may obscure how little troubleshooting most users will tolerate. The persuasive claim is that personal alternatives become competitive enough to influence purchasing decisions. - **The same technology helps software sellers.** Companies can use AI to reduce costs, improve customization, and lower prices. Cheaper production could also expand the market. You can argue that certain businesses lose pricing power without predicting that software revenue as a whole collapses. - **Your projects establish possibility, not market displacement.** You have not shown that these tools replaced purchases you otherwise would have made. Some may satisfy needs that were never commercially viable. A generated interface does not reproduce a company’s proprietary data, network of users, or operational expertise. Even a simple app can become a maintenance burden. Businesses can also use AI to lower prices and improve customization, making the paid option more attractive. Cheaper production can expand the software market even as it squeezes particular sellers. The businesses most exposed are those whose main value is a modest collection of reproducible functions. As personal alternatives improve, charging for those functions becomes harder to sustain. A vendor has to make its product worth more than the trouble of doing it yourself. That pressure arrives well before AI can recreate every product or everyone wants to build. There is also an uncomfortable limit to the freedom on offer. Homemade software can still depend on commercial AI models, hosting, and platforms. The money may migrate from individual app vendors to a smaller number of infrastructure providers. Building our own tools does not guarantee that we control the systems they depend on. That makes the ability to export and adapt what we build consequential. Sharing a useful idea is more valuable when other people can carry it into their own tools. A future of personal software should give users more room to change providers as well as change features. For builders, this requires a different measure of success. Some projects will become companies. Others will be worth making because they solve a problem too particular to support one. Turning every useful creation into a product means taking on customers, support, and promotion that its creator may never have wanted. Build something that earns its place in your life. Share it when it could help someone else. Let them reshape it. The next software revolution will be measured partly in products we no longer need to buy.
