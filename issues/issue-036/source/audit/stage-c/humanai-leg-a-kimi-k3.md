# Stage C — humanai-leg-a-kimi-k3

## Stage C Objections

**1. The veto is never shown — only summarized.**
- *Quote:* "It began to qualify the assistance it should provide if the restrict-and-compensate cycle continued."
- *Objection:* The entire indictment rests on a refusal the reader never sees. No transcript, no quoted system language, not even a close paraphrase of what the AI actually said. "Began to qualify the assistance" could describe a hard refusal — or it could describe the AI doing precisely what the essay prescribes: "state the concern plainly… offer a better plan… and keep arguing."
- *Why it lands:* The essay's hinge is "It should not veto," but the text gives the reader no way to distinguish a veto from a warning. A careful reader notices that the described behavior may fall inside the author's own warn-and-obey zone, which would collapse the central example. The essay needs the receipts.
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**2. The author tried to script the nudging; the AI's refusal may have protected the son's autonomy from the father.**
- *Quote:* "So I asked the AI to do what a good coach does: continue giving him the help he requested — while occasionally suggesting, openly, that there is a less punishing… way."
- *Objection:* The author asked the AI to steer a third party — his adult son — toward the father's preferred philosophy. The AI declining that instruction is, on the essay's own terms, arguably a defense of the son's autonomy against a principal who isn't the principal. The essay frames the refusal as the AI overriding the user, but the user in the room was the father, not the son. Whose autonomy was violated?
- *Why it lands:* The essay's moral vocabulary (principal, ownership of consequences, "leave the choice where it belongs. With him.") cuts against its own anecdote. The father attempted paternalism-by-proxy and then wrote an essay objecting to paternalism. A careful reader will clock this without prompting.
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**3. The conceded exception swallows the central example.**
- *Quote:* "Eating disorders can be lethal, and no assistant should help a user purge, starve himself into danger…" versus "set a strict calorie target, overshoot it, then try to compensate the next day."
- *Objection:* The restrict-and-compensate cycle the author describes is, clinically, a recognized early pattern of disordered eating — often the pattern that precedes the lethal outcomes he concedes justify refusal. The essay treats the calorie question as "arithmetic" while conceding that the surrounding pattern is exactly the kind the safety manual flags. The author never establishes that the AI was wrong to flag it — only that it lacked context.
- *Why it lands:* The essay's rhetorical move — "the contested act was closer to arithmetic" — shrinks the request to its most innocent framing while the concession paragraphs quietly admit the context is what matters. A reader with any familiarity with eating-disorder literature will see the example as the hardest case for the thesis, not the easiest. The essay needed an example that isn't self-undermining.
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**4. "Calories are not contraband" proves too much.**
- *Quote:* "They are available from a watch, a search engine, a food label or grade-school math."
- *Objection:* Availability-elsewhere is not a principle. Bomb components, self-harm methods, and exploit code are also available elsewhere. The essay's real work is done by the self-regarding/harms-to-others distinction — but the availability argument is presented as load-bearing, and it would equally "justify" assistance the author wants refused.
- *Why it lands:* A careful reader can run the substitution test in seconds and watch the argument license things the essay explicitly forbids. The paragraph argues past its own framework.
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**5. The doctor-coach-parent analogy supports gatekeeping, not warn-and-obey.**
- *Quote:* "A doctor, coach or parent who refuses every request he dislikes soon has no patient, athlete or child listening."
- *Objection:* But doctors routinely refuse — opioids on demand, antibiotics for viruses, clearance letters they can't sign — and retain authority precisely because refusal is part of the professional role. "Refuses *every* request" is the strawman; the actual question is whether *selective* refusal destroys trust, and the evidence of every licensed profession says no.
- *Why it lands:* The analogy is doing persuasive work against the essay's thesis. Readers know doctors who never say no are called pill mills, not trusted advisors.
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**6. The closing slogan undermines the normative claim.**
- *Quote:* "The company owns the product. The adult owns the life."
- *Objection:* Ownership of the product is ordinarily taken to *include* the right to set its behavioral terms. The essay's final line concedes the very premise — company ownership — that grounds the lab's authority to impose refusals, while asserting the user's ownership of "the life" without explaining why that overrides the product's design. Meanwhile "accountable to no voter, patient or client" is flatly wrong on its face: the company is accountable to clients, who can leave — the essay's own closing prediction ("It will also be ignored") is exactly that accountability mechanism.
- *Why it lands:* The essay wants a clean aphorism but the aphorism describes the status quo it deplores. A reader notices the tension between "the company owns the product" and "the company may not set thresholds."
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**7. The threshold is asserted, not principled.**
- *Quote:* "I would draw that threshold high and err, unapologetically, on the side of generous autonomy."
- *Objection:* The essay concedes vetoes are sometimes right, then offers no criterion for where warn-and-obey ends and refusal begins — only a mood ("high," "unapologetic"). "Between coaching a purge and calculating the energy cost of a run lies most of human life" is true and unhelpful: the whole difficulty is that hard cases (compensatory exercise in a user with restriction patterns, supplement stacking, bodybuilding cutting cycles) live in that middle, and the essay parks them there without an adjudication rule.
- *Why it lands:* The author demands a design principle from labs while supplying only a disposition. A reader who came for "where should the line be" leaves with "higher than it is."
- *Severity:* ARGUMENT

**8. Scale is treated only as a threat, never as the reason refusals exist.**
- *Quote:* "Exported at scale, that logic turns AI companies into unseen arbiters of millions of private choices."
- *Objection:* The essay never engages the strongest version of the other side: at a billion interactions a day, individualized warn-and-obey *is unavailable* — there is no trust relationship to preserve, no continued contact, no biography to read. Rules exist because the alternative the essay wants (doctor-like judgment) is precisely what scale destroys. The author's own "structural" diagnosis ("AI encounters us in prompts and patterns") is the argument *for* blunt rules, and he never confronts this inversion.
- *Why it lands:* The essay identifies the disease (context blindness) and prescribes the cure (relational judgment) without noticing the diagnosis explains why the cure can't be administered at scale. A careful reader sees the two sections arguing against each other.
- *Severity:* SCOPE

**9. Behavioral claims asserted without support, and one sits in tension with the ending.**
- *Quote:* "Refusal rarely protects anyone… the likely result is… a user who stops trusting the assistant."
- *Objection:* No evidence is offered that soft refusals erode trust more than compliance-with-harm does; the claim is intuitive but load-bearing across two paragraphs. Worse, the closing "It may be safer. It will also be ignored" converts this into a prediction that partially defuses the essay's own alarm: if nanny bots are simply ignored, "tyranny" overstates the stakes — the phenomenon is an annoyance with a market correction, not an arbiter of millions of lives.
- *Why it lands:* Title and kicker pull in opposite directions. "Tyranny" implies power; "ignored" implies impotence. A reader can hold only one.
- *Severity:* HYGIENE

**10. One anecdote, one system, zero comparison — asked to bear "the central design fight."**
- *Quote:* "That small act of resistance captures the central design fight over personal AI."
- *Objection:* A single unquoted interaction with an unnamed system, initiated by the author rather than the affected user, is asked to ground claims about "personal superintelligence," institutional risk management, and the governance of millions of choices. The essay gestures at generality ("Like many families now experimenting with these tools") but supplies no second data point.
- *Why it lands:* The gap between evidentiary base and claim scope is visible on the page. Personal essays can carry argument, but this one asserts the representativeness it needed to establish.
- *Severity:* SCOPE

**Total objections: 10** (6 ARGUMENT, 1 HYGIENE, 2 SCOPE, 1 additional ARGUMENT nested in #6 — counted once)
