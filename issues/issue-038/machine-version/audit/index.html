Stage C Critique: "What Intelligence Can't Tell You About Your Own Life"

Objections

1. The Terman claim rests on a vague hedge. Quote: "by most accounts their rates of divorce, alcoholism, and suicide were close to the national average." Objection: "By most accounts" is doing a lot of unexamined work. The Terman cohort had no proper control group, and published accounts of their adult outcomes genuinely differ — some report lower divorce rates than average, not parity. A careful reader who knows anything about this literature will notice the essay is selecting the account that fits its thesis and flagging the selection with a phrase designed to forestall checking. Why it lands: The Terman study is one of only two empirical anchors for the essay's central empirical claim (smart people aren't happier or better at life). If its support is "by most accounts," the anchor is weaker than the confident framing suggests. Severity: ARGUMENT

2. Elliot is one patient, and the inference is overextended. Quote: "Damasio concluded that emotions do work that reasoning cannot do alone." Objection: The essay moves from a single neurological case — a man with frontal lobe damage — to a general claim about how healthy, intelligent people should weight their emotions in choosing spouses and careers. The somatic marker framework underlying Damasio's interpretation has faced serious replication and methodological criticism (particularly around the Iowa Gambling Task). Even granting the case fully, "emotion is necessary for practical decision-making" does not entail "dread before a wedding is reliable information about what you need." Why it lands: The gap between the clinical finding and the everyday application is exactly the kind of inferential leap the essay itself warns intelligent people about. Severity: ARGUMENT

3. The essay never tells the reader when to trust emotion and when not to. Quote: "Like any evidence it can mislead, but a person who habitually overrides such signals... is discarding data." Objection: This concedes the problem and then walks away from it. If affective forecasts are unreliable (paragraph three) and self-theories are contradicted by behavior (same paragraph), why are pre-wedding dread and relief at a collapsed job offer trustworthy? The essay offers no criterion for distinguishing signal from noise in emotional evidence, yet its prescriptive sections lean heavily on treating feelings as data. Why it lands: This is the essay's central tension, and a careful reader will feel it: the destructive half of the argument undermines the constructive half. Severity: ARGUMENT

4. The "plainer evidence" recommended at the end is the same kind of evidence discredited earlier. Quote: "check the conclusion against plainer evidence, such as how you have been sleeping and whether you look forward to Monday." Objection: Sleep quality and Monday anticipation are self-reports and feelings — precisely the class of information the essay spent three paragraphs showing people misread, misremember, and filter through false self-theories. Calling them "plainer" doesn't make them more reliable; it just makes them less articulate. Why it lands: A reader tracking the argument's internal logic will notice the prescription re-imports the disease as the cure. Severity: ARGUMENT

5. The speed-date surrogate study is over-extrapolated. Quote: "women predicted how much they would enjoy a speed date more accurately when told how another woman had rated her date with the same man." Objection: Gilbert's surrogation finding concerns a low-stakes, minutes-long, near-identical experience across people. The essay uses it to license asking others about careers and life paths — decisions whose value plausibly does depend on idiosyncratic fit, which is the essay's own earlier premise ("you need to know what daily life with each would be like" for you). If individuals differ enough that self-theories fail, surrogates may fail too. Why it lands: The essay's skepticism about generalizing from profiles applies with equal force to generalizing from other people's experiences; it applies the skepticism selectively. Severity: ARGUMENT

6. The essay conflates "intelligent people" with "people who prize their reasoning." Quote: "people who prize their reasoning are often inclined to treat them as interference." Objection: The failure modes catalogued — dismissing emotion, rationalizing, trusting analysis over testimony — are described as belonging to the disposition of prizing reason. But the essay's target population is defined by test scores. No evidence is offered that high IQ causes or even correlates with that disposition; the Stanovich finding cited actually shows bias-blindness is uniform across ability levels, which cuts against the idea that intelligence specifically produces these liabilities. Why it lands: The title and framing promise a thesis about intelligence; the body repeatedly delivers a thesis about a temperament, and a reader will notice the substitution. Severity: ARGUMENT

7. The key premise about marriage and career choices is asserted, not supported. Quote: "Choices about whom to marry or what work to do turn mostly on that second kind of knowledge." Objection: "Mostly" is the load-bearing word and it has no evidence behind it. It's at least arguable that deliberative capacity matters substantially in mate and career choice — anticipating financial stress, recognizing patterns of conflict, evaluating long-term trade-offs. The essay needs this premise to reach its conclusion and simply states it. Why it lands: The entire thesis — intelligence is "a weaker asset here than its owners tend to expect" — stands on this unmoved mover. Severity: FLOOR

8. "Its owners tend to expect" is never established. Quote: "intelligence is a weaker asset here than its owners tend to expect." Objection: The essay's conclusion is comparative: intelligent people overestimate the usefulness of intelligence in personal life. But no evidence is offered that they do expect this — no survey, no observation, nothing. The essay critiques a belief it never demonstrates exists. Why it lands: A reader can accept everything in the essay and still note that the debunked expectation was invented for the occasion. Severity: ARGUMENT

9. The competence-trap mechanism is plausible but entirely conjectural. Quote: "A highly able person can perform well for twenty years at work that does not suit them, and every promotion looks like confirmation." Objection: This is the essay's most original claim and it arrives with zero evidence — no study, no observation, not even a "by most accounts." It's also conveniently unfalsifiable as stated: success is reinterpreted as a symptom of failure, which is exactly the kind of reasoning-against-evidence the essay warns against. Why it lands: The claim carries real weight in the essay's final warning ("a decade or more of succeeding at the wrong thing") and is supported only by its own rhetorical appeal. Severity: ARGUMENT

10. Solomon's paradox is stretched past its evidence. Quote: "asking what you would advise a friend in your position is a cheap way to get the same effect." Objection: The Grossmann–Kross finding cited concerns self-distancing — viewing one's own situation as an outsider. "What would you advise a friend?" is a different prompt with different dynamics (advice-giving invokes norms and idealizations, not just distance). The essay presents the extrapolation as if it were the studied result. Why it lands: Minor, but a reader familiar with the study — whom the essay invites by naming it — will register the slide from "the difference disappeared" for distancing to the advice frame. Severity: HYGIENE

Stage C — machine-leg-b-claude-opus-4.1

Objections

  1. Quote: "People who score higher on cognitive tests live longer on average, earn more, have fewer accidents, and are somewhat more willing to wait for a larger reward. They do not, however, report much greater satisfaction with their lives; that correlation is weak."

Objection: The essay treats life satisfaction self-reports as meaningful evidence while simultaneously arguing that people are poor at self-knowledge and self-reporting. Why it lands: The essay's core argument is that we're bad at knowing ourselves and predicting our feelings, yet it uncritically accepts satisfaction surveys as evidence that intelligence doesn't improve life outcomes. Severity: ARGUMENT

  1. Quote: "Lewis Terman's long-running study of about 1,500 high-IQ California children, begun in the 1920s, found that they went on to do well in school and work, yet by most accounts their rates of divorce, alcoholism, and suicide were close to the national average."

Objection: "Close to the national average" for negative life outcomes doesn't support the claim that intelligence provides limited help with personal decisions - it could mean intelligence helped them avoid worse outcomes given other factors. Why it lands: The essay presents this as evidence that intelligence doesn't help with personal decisions, but matching national averages doesn't establish this, especially without controlling for other variables. Severity: ARGUMENT

  1. Quote: "Emotions are among the best sources of that information, and people who prize their reasoning are often inclined to treat them as interference."

Objection: The essay assumes without evidence that valuing reasoning correlates with dismissing emotions, creating a false dichotomy between intelligence and emotional awareness. Why it lands: No evidence is provided that intelligent people systematically ignore emotions more than others, yet this assumption underlies much of the argument. Severity: ARGUMENT

  1. Quote: "A highly able person can perform well for twenty years at work that does not suit them, and every promotion looks like confirmation that the choice was sound."

Objection: This assumes promotions and performance are disconnected from suitability, but performing well for twenty years might indicate the work actually does suit them in important ways. Why it lands: The essay treats extended success as a trap rather than potential evidence of fit, without establishing why we should view it this way. Severity: FLOOR

  1. Quote: "In studies by Keith Stanovich and his colleagues, people with higher cognitive ability were no better than others at recognizing their own biases, and on some measures slightly worse."

Objection: The essay uses this to argue intelligence is a liability but doesn't address that being equally bad (or slightly worse) at bias recognition still leaves intelligent people with better overall decision-making given their other cognitive advantages. Why it lands: The essay implies this neutralizes intelligence advantages without considering that equal bias plus superior analysis might still yield better outcomes. Severity: ARGUMENT

  1. Quote: "Give more weight to the record of what you have done and felt than to your account of who you are"

Objection: The essay doesn't address how to interpret past behavior when circumstances, constraints, or personal growth may have changed what's possible or desirable now. Why it lands: Readers following this advice might over-anchor on past patterns that no longer apply, but the essay presents it as straightforward guidance. Severity: SCOPE
