# After the Congratulations
*A sharp mind can make an excellent case for what we want. It can also help us find out whether we want what comes with it.*

One odd thing about a promotion is that being offered it can make you want a job you didn’t want yesterday. Imagine a bridge engineer who likes designing things and has never particularly envied her boss. On Friday she’s asked to run the office. By Saturday she’s worked out what the raise would do for her mortgage and how she’d reorganize the team. These are good questions, and she’s good at them. It may be a while before she notices that one of the things she wants from the job—the knowledge that somebody thinks she can do it—is something she got on Friday.

If by intelligence we mean the ability to learn, reason and see connections, it plainly gives her an advantage. She can spot the clause in the contract that makes part of the raise less certain than it sounds. She might see a possibility nobody has offered: leading a smaller team while keeping a hand in design. Nor is intelligence just a calculator brought in after the heart has decided. Thinking through what she’d miss might reveal, for the first time, how much it matters to her to be able to point out a train window and say, “I worked on that.” Reasoning can bring that value to light, though it can’t establish what the pleasure ought to be worth to her.

Consider what people mean when they say they need the money. If the engineer is paying for her mother’s care, she has a reason for taking the job that comes due monthly, regardless of how she feels about management. If she can’t bear to earn less than her younger brother, what the raise buys her will depend rather precariously on his next salary review. That doesn’t make the wish for recognition contemptible; it does make a raise an uncertain way to satisfy it. Nor must every desire pass itself off as a need to deserve respect. Living near an old friend is hardly a necessity in the way that paying the rent is. The evenings spent in that friend’s kitchen might still be an excellent reason not to move.

Feelings can correct the account we give of what matters to us. A man contemplating marriage might speak with perfect sincerity about his fiancée’s wit and generosity, yet notice that what he feels when she cancels dinner is relief. It would be foolish to discount the relief and equally foolish to treat it as an instruction to break up. Does he dread her company, or the prospect of anybody’s company after a shift on the hospital ward? How does he feel on a Sunday morning when she lets herself in and puts the coffee on? Here intelligence helps by distinguishing two explanations that point toward quite different lives. If the relief persists even on restful Sundays, he owes them both a conversation.

The catch for an ingenious person is how easy it can be to explain away a whole year’s worth of Sundays. In an experiment by the psychologist Keith Stanovich and his colleagues, American students considering whether to ban a car on safety grounds were more inclined to favor the ban when it was a German car on American roads than when it was an American car on German roads. Higher scores on measures of cognitive ability did little to diminish that tilt. The lesson isn’t that clever people are especially biased. It’s that being able to make the case for the other side is different from being disposed to give it a hearing. If you can produce a persuasive argument on demand, the experience of having produced one tells you rather little.

A useful discipline, then, is to seek evidence you don’t get to compose. Our engineer could ask an experienced manager what makes leadership rewarding and get a thoughtful answer about mentoring young designers. Or she could ask the manager to open her calendar to last Thursday. The answer about mentoring might be entirely sincere, and it might still turn out that most of Thursday went to disputing an invoice and finding cover for a colleague on leave. Better yet, the engineer might stand in for her boss for two weeks. Does she still enjoy being the person everyone comes to on day nine, when nobody is making a fuss about it?

Other people matter as more than sources of evidence. If the new role involves travel, the engineer might explain to her partner how they could cover school pickups, only to discover that what he minds about the arrangement is the missed family dinners. Their daughter seldom volunteers much about her day before the second helping; those meals are when they hear what’s on her mind. The quicker thinker in a household can win the argument about logistics before the person across the table has had a chance to say what he wants to keep. Part of choosing wisely is declining that easy victory.

Even the most candid inquiry has a stopping point. Some choices, like becoming a parent or taking up a vocation, help form the very preferences we’re trying to consult. We can’t ask our present selves for a reliable report on an attachment that doesn’t yet exist. And waiting for certainty has consequences of its own: children grow up, offers expire, a friend moves away. It can be wise to make a commitment whose rewards are uncertain if we understand its demands, have listened to those who will share them, and see in it something worth doing even on a day when it doesn’t feel rewarding.

Suppose the engineer takes the job. One morning, long after the congratulations have stopped, a young designer turns up at her door with a drawing that isn’t working. She sees the fix in thirty seconds. What the job asks of her, though, is to leave the pencil in his hand and pull up a chair. The raise was a figure she could work out on Saturday. Whether she wanted to pull up that chair was a harder question, and one just as deserving of her very good mind.
