## 1. The thesis

> Because intelligence makes you a better advocate for whatever you already want, the smartest thing a smart person can do when desire is on the line is distrust their own reasoning and defer to inherited wisdom.

Your opening line (smart people fool themselves better) is the familiar half of this. The sharper half is buried in your last paragraph: the prescription and its trigger, "when wants, needs, and desires are involved." That trigger is what keeps the piece from being generic traditionalism, so I built on it.

## 2. Pressure-test

1. **Your central claim is overstated.** "The opposite appears to be true" is false as written, since higher IQ predicts better health, income, and longevity. The research supports something narrower: intelligence does nothing to reduce motivated reasoning and gives it better ammunition. The fix is to call intelligence an amplifier, not a handicap, and confine the argument to decisions where desire is involved.

2. **Your two examples can't carry the argument.**
   - **I can't verify them.** I can't open links and can't confirm either story from what I know. Allegations about a named person's private life need on-the-record sourcing, so I left both out.
   - **They don't isolate intelligence.** Plenty of people of average intelligence have affairs; power and opportunity explain that better than IQ. You need cases where the reasoning itself caused the failure.
   - **The "we'd have institutions for it" argument proves too much.** It would have condemned every new practice in history, including marrying for love. It also drags the piece into a fight over consenting adults' private lives, which would swallow your thesis.

3. **"Traditions survived because they improve lives" is survivorship logic.** Slavery, foot-binding, and bloodletting survived for centuries too. Traditions also persist because they serve the powerful, and every moral advance began with someone refusing to defer. The fix is to argue for a presumption rather than a veto, stick to near-universal rules, and give readers a test for telling the reformer from the rationalizer.

4. **The argument eats itself.** You are using a clever argument to tell people to distrust clever arguments, and "use your intellect to decide when to overrule it" sounds circular. The fix is precommitment: you decide in advance, with a clear head, which rules you won't relitigate in the heat of wanting.

## 4. The draft

**Smart Enough to Fool Yourself**

In March 2022, the economist Tyler Cowen put a thought experiment to Sam Bankman-Fried. Suppose you were offered a bet: a 51 percent chance of creating a second Earth, a 49 percent chance that this one disappears. Would you take it? And keep taking it, double or nothing? Bankman-Fried, an MIT physics graduate then worth billions, said he would.

Eight months later his crypto exchange collapsed. A jury went on to convict him of defrauding his customers, whose deposits had been funneled into his own trading firm's bets, and he was sentenced to 25 years in prison. Any grandmother could have told him what his math could not: don't bet what you can't afford to lose, and never bet what isn't yours.

We treat stories like this as paradoxes. How could someone so smart be so foolish? The question assumes that intelligence guards against self-deception. More often it serves it: the smarter you are, the better the case you can build for whatever you already wanted. That is an awkward fact for an age that venerates intelligence, hands its brightest people fortunes and cultural authority, and is spending hundreds of billions of dollars to manufacture more of it. And it points to an unfashionable conclusion. When your own desires are on the line, the smartest thing you can do is trust your intellect less, and the accumulated wisdom of the people who came before you more.

Start with how reasoning works. The psychologist Jonathan Haidt argues in "The Righteous Mind" that the intellect behaves less like a scientist searching for truth than like a lawyer hired to defend a verdict the client has already reached. Desire decides; reasoning writes the brief. A higher IQ just buys you a better lawyer. Haidt cites the Harvard researcher David Perkins, who asked people to generate arguments on contested issues and found that IQ strongly predicted how many they produced, but only for their own side. People, Perkins concluded, "invest their IQ in buttressing their own case rather than in exploring the entire issue more fully and evenhandedly." Keith Stanovich, who has studied this for decades, finds that the tendency to bend evidence toward what we already believe is essentially unrelated to intelligence. Smart people are as biased as everyone else. They are simply more convincing, above all to themselves.

The historical record reads accordingly. Long-Term Capital Management, a hedge fund whose partners included two Nobel laureates, ran models that rated the losses it eventually suffered as all but impossible. In 1998 it lost $4.6 billion in a matter of months and had to be rescued by a consortium of banks assembled by the Federal Reserve. Linus Pauling, the only person to win two unshared Nobel Prizes, spent his final decades insisting that megadoses of vitamin C could treat cancer, long after clinical trials said otherwise. Isaac Newton lost a fortune in the South Sea Bubble. In none of these cases was intelligence the brake that failed. It was the engine.

If your own reasoning can't be trusted when you want something, what can? Often, the very rules you are tempted to reason your way around.

The anthropologist Joseph Henrich describes how indigenous peoples of the Amazon process bitter manioc, a staple crop laced with cyanide, through a laborious, multi-day routine of scraping, grating, washing and waiting. The reasons for each step are invisible to the people performing them. A clever skeptic could skip half the steps, save hours of labor and feel perfectly fine for years, because the poisoning that results is slow and cumulative. When manioc was carried to Africa without the routine, chronic cyanide poisoning followed, and it persists in places today. The tradition was smarter than anyone who followed it, and far smarter than anyone clever enough to optimize it.

Our inherited rules about fidelity, honesty, debt and risk are manioc routines. They are the residue of millions of lives' worth of trial and error, compressed into the word "don't." You may not be able to derive them from first principles. That says less about the rules than about first principles. G.K. Chesterton's advice still holds: before you tear down a fence, be able to say why someone built it.

To be sure, tradition has an ugly record of its own. Slavery was a tradition. So was foot-binding. So was bleeding the sick. Every moral advance began with someone refusing to defer. And intelligence remains, on average, a blessing: people with higher IQs tend to live longer, healthier, more prosperous lives.

But the claim here is narrower than "obey your ancestors." It is a conflict-of-interest rule. A brilliant judge still recuses herself from a case in which she has a stake, because a stake bends even brilliant judgment. The reformers who overturned bad traditions argued in public, for principles that applied to everyone, and withstood decades of hostile cross-examination. The rationalizer argues in private, for an exemption that applies to himself, starting tonight. When your brilliant argument concludes that the old rule doesn't apply to you, and that conclusion happens to deliver exactly what you wanted, be suspicious. That is your lawyer talking.

Homer understood this. Odysseus was the cleverest of the Greeks, and his cleverest act was an admission that cleverness has limits. Warned about the Sirens by someone who knew those waters, he made no plan to out-argue them. He knew that once the song began, every argument his magnificent mind produced would be an argument for steering toward the rocks. So he decided in advance, while his head was clear, and had himself tied to the mast.

That is the kind of intelligence we are about to need. We are building machines that can produce a persuasive case for anything, on demand; soon everyone will have a brilliant lawyer on retainer for whatever they want to believe. Raw intelligence is about to be abundant. The scarce thing, the one that separates good lives from spectacular wrecks, will be knowing when to stop listening to it and reach for the rope.
