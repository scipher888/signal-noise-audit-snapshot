## 1. Core thesis

**Intelligence can make a self-serving exception sound like a matter of principle, especially to the person arguing for it—which is why brilliance is no license to be the judge in your own case.**

## 2. Pressure-test

- **Cut the opening claim as written.** “Smart people can rationalize” does not establish “the smarter you are, the worse your judgment.” Intelligence can also help people detect errors. Your better target is the assumption that a well-reasoned defense must be the product of an impartial inquiry.
- **Separate intelligence from power.** IQ, expertise, wealth and status are not four names for the same advantage. The sharpest point in your discussion of success is that promotion can remove a check on bad judgment. The engineer who becomes the boss need not grow more biased; she may simply lose the colleague who could overrule her.
- **Cut both personal anecdotes, not just the names.** Even if the underlying conduct were verified, it would not tell you whether anyone misunderstood a risk or knowingly accepted it. Nor can you infer fulfillment from someone’s private life, or a security threat from someone’s nationality. Neither example can carry the argument you’ve assigned it.
- **Make tradition earn its place.** An institution can survive because it solves a recurring problem or because it serves people with the power to preserve it. Conversely, the absence of an institution devoted to a practice tells us little about the value of that practice. Defend one inherited safeguard whose purpose you can demonstrate.
- **Take the strongest objection seriously: the interested expert may be right.** Handing the decision to an ignorant outsider is no victory for judgment. The remedy is to separate the authority to make the case from the authority to grant the exception—not to “subjugate” anyone’s intellect.

## 3. Op-ed structure

- **Lede:** One share of stock can disqualify the country’s finest patent judge from hearing a patent case. Why don’t we let her argue that she can be fair?
- **Nut graph:** As AI builders are asked both to explain their products and to judge what risks others should accept, it matters that expertise and impartiality are different qualifications.
- **Evidence and fortification:** Keep three elements from the draft: Haidt’s account of intellect as advocate, tested against research on “myside bias”; the observation that status changes who can challenge you; and the insight that inherited rules can protect us from predictable errors, illustrated by judicial recusal and a household promise.
- **To be sure:** Experts sometimes spot defects in rules that outsiders miss. Invite them to propose a better standard—one they would accept for a rival, too.
- **Kicker:** Decide who gets to tell you no before you discover how badly you want a yes.

## 4. The draft

### You Can Be Right and Still Be the Wrong Person to Decide

One share of stock can disqualify a federal judge from hearing a case. She might be the best patent judge in the country; the share might be worth less than a used copy of her book on patent law. If she owns it directly in a company that is a party to the suit, [federal law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/455) generally requires her to step aside. We don’t invite her to write a brief explaining why she can be trusted to make an exception. Imagine how good that brief would be.

Outside the courthouse, we sometimes accept just such a brief as the verdict. As companies sell artificial intelligence to hospitals and law firms, the person best equipped to explain why a flaw in a product isn’t serious may also be the founder whose fortune rides on getting it to market. Her stake doesn’t make her a worse engineer. It does make “listen to her” and “let her decide” two very different propositions.

In *The Righteous Mind*, psychologist Jonathan Haidt describes much of our moral reasoning as a lawyer’s work on behalf of an intuition that has already picked a side. A conscientious lawyer needn’t invent a single fact. She can find a real weakness in a witness’s account, an overlooked precedent, an ambiguity in the law. Every sentence in the brief can be true. What’s missing is the work she would have done, with equal diligence, had the other side retained her.

That’s a subtler problem than the tempting claim that the brighter you are, the worse your judgment. In a [2013 review of research on “myside bias,”](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721413480174) psychologists Keith Stanovich, Richard West and Maggie Toplak describe an experiment in which Americans considered a proposed vehicle ban on safety grounds. They were readier to keep a dangerous German vehicle off American roads than to endorse an equivalent German ban on an American vehicle. Across a range of tasks, the researchers reported, the tendency to favor one’s own side bore little relation to measured intelligence. Knowing someone’s test score tells you surprisingly little about whether they’ll apply the same standard when the flags change.

Success, though, can change what happens to that bias. A junior engineer who thinks a failed test shouldn’t hold up a launch has to persuade somebody. Give her enough promotions and she may have exactly the same impulse and exactly the same arguments, except now the people who disagree report to her. Looking back on a career of good calls, how many of us remember to count the bad ones a colleague kept us from making?

Suppose an AI company builds a tool to help lawyers write briefs. Six months ago, before taking orders, the team agreed to delay release if the tool kept citing court decisions that didn’t exist. Now the first customer has signed and the tool has failed its last test. The founder notices something important: every invented citation came in response to a question about the law in a state where the customer has no clients. Why should a firm in Ohio wait for a fix to a problem in Alaska?

The founder might be right. We should want her to make that case in all its technical detail. What we shouldn’t want is for her to change the test on Tuesday so her company can say it passed on Wednesday. Give her objection and the data to someone equipped to evaluate both, whose pay doesn’t depend on the sale, and who can postpone the launch. An adviser the founder is free to ignore is just somebody to thank in the press release.

To be sure, bad rules have staying power, too. Professional associations excluded women for generations; one thing an institution can preserve is a good deal for its members. If the company’s test is a bad test, change it. But ask the founder to state the new standard in terms she would accept for a competitor whose product she distrusts. Would she be content to have that company use it first? There’s a difference between proposing a better rule and asking for a dispensation that expires the moment you’ve got yours.

At home, the person who deserves a say may be the one with just as much at stake as you. Suppose you and your spouse agreed to consult each other before lending money to relatives. Then your brother calls in a jam and, by the time he hangs up, you’ve worked out that what he needs is really more of an *advance*. After all, he gets paid Friday. You may have excellent reasons to lend him the money. None is a reason not to make the second phone call. That promise was made for the day you could see no point in keeping it.

The wisdom worth borrowing from the courthouse is a matter of timing. Settle who gets to tell you no before you discover how badly you want a yes. By the time you ask for an exception, you’ll have reasons. The danger is that some of them will be excellent.
