# Stage C — humanai-leg-b-claude-opus-4.1

## Objections

### 1. Selective Evidence
**Quote**: "Consider recent *Washington Post* reporting on Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, widely regarded as one of the most formidable strategic minds in modern business. According to the report, Huang engaged in extramarital affairs..."

**Objection**: The essay builds its central claim on a handful of cherry-picked scandals while ignoring the vast majority of highly intelligent people who don't make such mistakes. Where is the systematic evidence that smart people are actually *more* prone to these failures than average?

**Why it lands**: A careful reader would notice the essay makes a sweeping claim ("The Smartest People...Are the Easiest to Fool") but provides only anecdotal evidence. The selection bias undermines the argument's foundation.

**Severity**: ARGUMENT

### 2. Contradictory Scope Claims
**Quote**: "To be sure, you don't need a sky-high IQ to blow up a marriage or abuse power; mediocre men in corner offices have been doing both since the dawn of commerce."

**Objection**: This admission directly contradicts the title and central thesis. If mediocre people have always done these things, how are the smartest people "the easiest" to fool?

**Why it lands**: The essay wants to have it both ways—claiming high IQ makes you uniquely vulnerable while acknowledging the same failures happen across all intelligence levels.

**Severity**: ARGUMENT

### 3. Undefined Key Comparison
**Quote**: "An ordinary person produces an excuse. A gifted person produces a philosophy."

**Objection**: This distinction is asserted without evidence. How does the author know ordinary people don't also create elaborate justifications? The essay provides no comparative analysis of rationalization quality across intelligence levels.

**Why it lands**: The reader is asked to accept a key differentiator between smart and average people based purely on assertion, weakening the essay's explanatory power.

**Severity**: ARGUMENT

### 4. Circular Definition of "Wisdom"
**Quote**: "When your bold rejection of a long-standing guardrail just so happens to flatter your ego, indulge your appetites, or exempt you from accountability, you aren't pioneering the future..."

**Objection**: The essay defines wisdom circularly as "following traditional norms when they prevent bad outcomes" without explaining how to identify which traditions deserve preservation before consequences manifest. This makes the argument unfalsifiable.

**Why it lands**: A careful reader would notice the essay offers no forward-looking method to distinguish valuable traditions from outdated ones, only retrospective judgment.

**Severity**: ARGUMENT

### 5. Unexamined Survivorship Bias
**Quote**: "The habits, taboos, and institutions preserved across generations—fidelity, institutional accountability, durable family and civic bonds—are not arbitrary rules...They are the distilled, battle-tested residue of millions of human experiments..."

**Objection**: The essay assumes that traditions survive because they work, ignoring that many persist through power structures, path dependence, or simple inertia. Where is the acknowledgment that many "battle-tested" traditions (slavery, monarchy, religious persecution) endured for millennia despite being harmful?

**Why it lands**: The reader would recognize this as survivorship bias—assuming what exists must be optimal simply because it exists.

**Severity**: ARGUMENT

### 6. Conflation of Domains
**Quote**: "Human flourishing, however, is not a technical optimization problem."

**Objection**: The essay treats "human flourishing" as a monolithic category opposed to technical domains, but many human problems (public health, education, economic systems) have benefited enormously from technical, first-principles thinking. The binary is too simplistic.

**Why it lands**: Readers familiar with improvements in human welfare through technical approaches would find this dichotomy artificially narrow.

**Severity**: SCOPE

**Total objections: 6**
