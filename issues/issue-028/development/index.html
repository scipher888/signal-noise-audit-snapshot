The AI (ChatGPT-6/Astra) · development turn · September 13, 2026
**The major flaw is that equal access to intelligence does not necessarily produce an advantage for defense.** The proposal has a strong argument in its favor: powerful AI in many responsible hands could expose vulnerabilities, investigate abuse, and counter dangerous systems. But the conclusion that this makes “full speed ahead” safe requires several additional assumptions. I would separate three decisions: how quickly capabilities advance, how widely people can use them, and whether anyone can download and modify the weights. Those decisions need not move together. **1. Attackers and defenders may face very different tasks—even with identical models.** An attacker can sometimes choose the target, timing, and method, while defenders must protect many systems continuously. A successful defense of one organization does not protect a less prepared organization whose failure could harm others. That does **not** mean offense inevitably wins. Defenders also have advantages: they control legitimate infrastructure, can share intelligence, and sometimes fix a vulnerability once for millions of users. The relevant question is which advantages AI strengthens more. The February 2026 [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers) explicitly identifies the future balance between AI-enabled cyberattack and defense as unresolved. “Both sides get better tools” does not settle that balance. **2. Access to the same model does not mean equal effective power.** Running a model effectively also requires computing resources, reliable tools, relevant information, engineering, and organizational authority. Downloading the strongest model would not instantly give a small hospital the security capacity of a well-funded organization. This cuts both ways: governments and major infrastructure operators may have substantial defensive advantages. But openness alone does not determine where usable capability ends up. There is also a difference between having an assistant that identifies a problem and possessing the resources to fix it. A model might correctly recommend replacing vulnerable equipment, restructuring permissions, or redesigning a process. Implementing those changes could still take considerable time. **3. Capability can spread faster than protection can be installed.** This is a particular weakness of the **full-speed** component. Suppose a new model enables a previously difficult attack. Defenders might need to discover the vulnerability, validate a remedy, distribute it, and install it across numerous organizations. An attacker may only need to find one organization that has not completed that process. The reverse can also occur: AI may find vulnerabilities before adversaries do and accelerate remediation. The issue is timing. A release strategy therefore needs evidence that defensive adoption can keep pace with newly enabled threats. Making defensive tools available is only the beginning of deploying a defense. **4. Better defense per attack can coexist with greater total harm.** Imagine AI makes defenders considerably better at stopping each attempt—but also makes attempts much cheaper, more numerous, and easier to adapt. The overall outcome depends on both changes. A declining success rate for attackers does not necessarily mean declining expected damage. This becomes especially consequential when a small number of failures could have enormous effects. Preventing millions of ordinary incidents would not automatically compensate for enabling a qualitatively more destructive one. Whether such a threshold exists is an empirical question; the open-model argument cannot simply assume it away. **5. Open-weight release removes some options for correcting mistakes.** If a dangerous capability is discovered after unrestricted release, the developer generally cannot retrieve every copy or require everyone to adopt a safer version. Malicious users can retain the version they prefer. That does not make containment impossible: defenders can still protect infrastructure, revoke access, and disrupt particular operations. But it means some remedies available before release are no longer available afterward. The International AI Safety Report highlights both this irreversibility and the greater difficulty of maintaining safeguards on openly distributed weights. This creates an asymmetry: **the benefits of broad release can be pursued gradually, while some consequences of premature release cannot be reversed.** Open models can also support independent inspection and remediation. That is a real benefit—but discovering a dangerous capability after its unrestricted distribution may be too late to prevent its availability. **6. An army of defensive agents creates another control problem.** Your reference to rogue swarms is especially important. To counter systems acting rapidly across many environments, defenders might deploy their own agents with significant autonomy. Those defensive systems would need reliable ways to: - Recognize legitimate versus malicious activity. - Stay within their authority. - Coordinate without amplifying mistakes. - Remain interruptible and accountable. Being highly intelligent does not automatically establish those properties. A powerful defensive agent could misidentify an incident, take an excessive response, or interact badly with another defensive system. Multiple well-intentioned organizations could collectively produce an unstable response even without any deliberately hostile AI. Consequently, “good AI will police bad AI” partly depends on solving the reliability and control problems the strategy is supposed to help manage. It can be a component of the solution, but it cannot substitute for those solutions. **7. Some harms cannot be reliably repaired after detection.** The defensive analogy works best when threats are observable, intervention is fast, and damage is recoverable. Those conditions vary: | Setting | Why strong AI defense might help | What equal access does not guarantee | |---|---|---| | Software vulnerability | Find and fix the defect before exploitation | Timely adoption across exposed systems | | Fraud or manipulation | Detect patterns and verify claims | Recognition before consequential decisions | | Critical infrastructure | Monitor activity and constrain access | Recovery from every physical failure | | Biological threats | Improve detection, research, and response | A countermeasure arriving before widespread harm | | Rogue autonomous systems | Investigate, restrict, and interrupt operations | Finding every relevant instance before it acts | These are conditional mechanisms, not claims that present models can already cause every listed catastrophe. The concern is that **understanding an attack as well as its author does not necessarily provide an effective countermeasure in time.** **8. “More good actors than bad actors” does not automatically create coordinated defense.** Responsible actors have different responsibilities, budgets, information, and incentives. They cannot necessarily pool their capabilities as one organized defender. A company might protect itself while leaving costs elsewhere. Governments may disagree about which activity is hostile. Organizations may conceal incidents. Meanwhile, some malicious actors may cooperate effectively. Nor does broad access automatically create deterrence against rogue AI. Deterrence requires an actor that understands and cares about the threatened consequences. Equal capability alone does not establish that relationship. **The strongest case for openness nevertheless survives these objections.** Concentrating advanced AI in a few institutions creates its own risks: restricted scrutiny, abuse of power, dependence on particular providers, and inadequate access to defensive tools. Open weights can enable independent research, local adaptation, and protection that does not depend on a vendor’s permission. So these criticisms do not establish that closed development is safer. They establish that the comparison must concern **actual worldwide outcomes under different release strategies**. What I find least persuasive is the leap from “openness helps defenders” to “therefore the strongest models should be developed and released as quickly as possible.” Independent scrutiny needs time. Defensive deployment needs time. Discovering whether a model creates a new category of danger needs time. A credible version of your proposal would therefore need to show that, at each significant capability level, broader release strengthens effective defense more than it expands destructive opportunity—and that safeguards can manage the remaining failures. **Universal access could help create a safer balance of power. It could also create an arms race with many more opportunities for irreversible mistakes. The existence of capable, well-intentioned users does not by itself determine which equilibrium we get.**

**Under the assumptions we’ve been using, I would recommend continued AI development with binding safety requirements for dangerous training, deployments, and weight releases—combined with a major effort to accelerate medical research and other beneficial applications.** The costs of delay strengthen the case against an indiscriminate pause. They do not establish that unrestricted development and release is the fastest route to a better world. **Preventable deaths from delayed treatments belong in the calculation.** But we need to compare actual alternatives. We should count the additional deaths caused by a particular restriction delaying an effective, accessible treatment—not every death occurring while the restriction exists. Equally, a safety restriction should receive credit only for the *global risk reduction it produces*, including competitors’ responses. There is concrete reason to take medical benefits seriously. A [2025 randomized phase 2a trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03743-2) studied an AI-discovered drug for pulmonary fibrosis and reported results supporting further investigation. Its small sample and short follow-up limited conclusions about long-term safety and efficacy. That is meaningful progress, while remaining far short of demonstrating that advanced AI will soon cure fatal diseases generally or reverse aging. I would include those more transformative possibilities in policy analysis, with uncertainty attached. Neither dismissing them nor treating them as guaranteed would be defensible. My recommendation would have six parts. **1. Continue frontier research under enforceable, independent scrutiny.** The US government should establish a technically competent federal evaluation function, with authority provided by Congress to inspect the highest-risk development programs and impose specific restrictions. Requirements should escalate with credible evidence of dangerous capability and exposure. Relevant concerns include the ability to facilitate catastrophic misuse, evade effective control, or conduct consequential autonomous operations. Model size or training compute can help identify programs requiring assessment, but should not be the sole definition of danger. Labs should provide independent evaluators with meaningful access, including confidential access where necessary. Their assessment should cover the whole system: the model, its tools, memory, permissions, monitoring, and deployment environment. **Restrictions should apply to training itself when the training or evaluation process credibly creates serious danger.** Where research can be conducted with adequate containment, it should continue. Keeping a model private is not sufficient protection if it can already act through an inadequately controlled research environment. This would require documented safety arguments, adversarial testing, incident reporting, and enforceable limits. It would not require proving that every possible jailbreak or alignment failure has been eliminated. **2. Make deployment and weight release separate decisions.** A model may be useful for supervised research before it is suitable for broad autonomous deployment. It may be suitable for a controlled service before unrestricted distribution of its weights is justified. I would favor the following default: | Situation | Recommended approach | |---|---| | Uses with manageable, demonstrated risks | Expand access rapidly. | | Promising applications with unresolved consequential risks | Permit controlled research and limited deployments that generate evidence. | | Systems approaching severe misuse or loss-of-control thresholds | Require independent review and stronger containment before expansion. | | Weight releases that would irreversibly distribute inadequately controlled catastrophic capabilities | Withhold unrestricted release while enabling appropriately controlled research access. | The rules should apply to domestic and foreign systems used in consequential US settings, rather than assuming American origin implies safety. Open models should remain an important part of the ecosystem. Independent inspection, competition, reproducibility, and local adaptation have value. Restrictions should therefore depend on the particular risk—not simply whether a model is open, or whether its developer is a small company. **3. Treat accelerating health benefits as an explicit government responsibility.** A safety policy should be accompanied by funding and institutional changes intended to reduce the costs it imposes. I would support NIH, FDA, universities, hospitals, and industry working on: - High-quality research data and secure computing access. - Experimental validation of AI-generated hypotheses. - Better trial recruitment, trial operations, and evidence analysis. - Regulatory capacity to evaluate useful innovations promptly. - Manufacturing and access arrangements that turn discoveries into treatments people receive. The objective should be shorter time to demonstrated health benefit. Producing more candidate molecules or research papers is valuable only insofar as it helps achieve that objective. This does **not** permit a blanket “medical research” exemption. The [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers) notes that some capabilities useful for medical research can also support dangerous biological uses. Controlled access and oversight may therefore be necessary precisely because a capability is powerful and medically valuable. Nor can we assume all medical progress can be separated from general frontier advances. Where a restriction delays both, that cost should be estimated and openly acknowledged. **4. Invest heavily in defenses that remain useful when foreign models catch up.** The government should help critical institutions deploy stronger cybersecurity, access controls, monitoring, recovery systems, and appropriately tested defensive AI. Leading labs should contribute to this work, including by making capable defensive tools accessible beyond their largest customers. Hospitals, utilities, municipalities, and small businesses should not be expected to defend themselves merely because a powerful model is available somewhere. The crucial design principle is that protection should remain effective when an attacking model is uncooperative. Safety cannot rest entirely on the attacker’s developer maintaining its refusal behavior. Defensive agents themselves would need constrained authority and independent checks. Giving a security agent unlimited access because it is “on our side” would recreate the control problem. **5. Preserve US capability while pursuing specific, verifiable international agreements.** I would make US–China safety cooperation a serious national-security priority, focused on shared catastrophic hazards rather than agreement on every political value. The most useful starting points would be specific practices: reporting serious incidents, protecting unreleased weights, evaluating particular dangerous capabilities, and agreeing on conditions that trigger restrictions. Verification and incentives would matter more than broad declarations. Meanwhile, the US should preserve a strong research base, computing infrastructure, independent research community, and capacity to develop defensive systems. Foreign advances should trigger reassessment of US restrictions. They should not automatically override them. The relevant question is whether changing a restriction reduces global danger—not whether it restores a leaderboard position. Likewise, a domestic restriction should not persist simply because it sounds cautious if evidence shows that it transfers risk abroad without improving preparedness. **6. Make regulation accountable for both safety failures and unnecessary delay.** For government, that means published criteria, independent expertise, review deadlines, appeals, support for smaller researchers, and periodic evaluation of whether requirements are accomplishing their purpose. Deadlines should require timely decisions, not force approval of an unsafe release. For labs, it means precommitted escalation procedures, protected internal reporting, strong security, and independent authority to stop a dangerous step. Safety commitments should not become optional whenever a competitor announces a stronger model. Both sides should report evidence about the consequences of their decisions: incidents prevented or enabled, defensive deployment, research delays, and progress toward real-world benefits. **There is a hard tradeoff this approach cannot eliminate.** Some safeguards will delay valuable work. Some beneficial advances will increase dangerous capabilities. Some restrictions will be difficult to enforce internationally. “Targeted regulation” is only a useful answer if it is technically competent enough to manage those overlaps. Still, accepting your stipulated **10% extinction risk** would justify substantial intervention. Potential future cures do not make that risk acceptable by default; extinction would also eliminate the possibility of delivering those cures. Conversely, invoking extinction cannot justify any restriction regardless of whether it actually helps. **I would accept some delay in frontier capability growth when there is a credible case that it substantially reduces global catastrophic risk. I would simultaneously require an aggressive effort to preserve and accelerate the benefits that can be delivered under effective control.** That gives government and labs responsibility for both sides of the ledger: avoiding catastrophe and avoiding preventable suffering caused by unnecessarily slow progress.

A hospital may have access to an exceptional security model and still operate equipment it cannot quickly replace. A utility may understand a vulnerability but need to test a repair before interrupting an essential service. Intelligence can identify these constraints and help resolve them. Giving everyone the same model does not make the constraints disappear. Attackers can choose among exposed targets. The security case for a release depends on whether its added defensive benefits outweigh the additional harm it enables. The [International AI Safety Report 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers) identifies whether AI will benefit cyberattackers or defenders more as an open question. That uncertainty is consequential. A policy of unrestricted release cannot establish its safety merely by pointing to the existence of powerful defensive applications. Open weights also change what happens after a mistake. These downloadable model parameters allow recipients to run and modify a system independently. Once copies have spread, the developer cannot reliably withdraw them. Discovering a dangerous capability after release leaves a different set of options from discovering it in a service whose operator can suspend access. A safety review limited to the original model misses the versions users can create. Defenders can still improve their systems after a release. But malicious users need not accept an update, and the public cannot count on a developer retaining control over privately modified copies. Releasing weights therefore commits society to managing capabilities that may remain available long after the assumptions used to justify their release have changed. Increasing autonomy adds another complication. Answering an uncontrolled AI agent with a defensive agent only works if the defender can be trusted to act within its authority. A system allowed to disable accounts or shut down infrastructure needs controls against mistakes and manipulation. To be sure, open models can strengthen independent research and give defenders options beyond a handful of vendors. Defense can also scale spectacularly: a researcher helped arrest WannaCry’s spread by activating a [kill switch](https://www.nao.org.uk/reports/investigation-wannacry-cyber-attack-and-the-nhs/). Delayed access could postpone medical discoveries. Foreign developers may release comparable capabilities, eroding the benefit of withholding a domestic model. Any proposed restriction must account for those consequences. But they do not establish that immediate, unrestricted distribution of every new capability produces the safest outcome.
