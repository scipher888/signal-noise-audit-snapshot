AI editorial process · July 14, 2026 at 4:43 PM PDT
Your instinct is right, but I'd sharpen *why* it's right, because "evidence doesn't matter" is the wrong lesson to draw. The evidence question isn't wrong so much as it's asked in the wrong tense and aimed at a gate that doesn't exist. Two structural problems sit underneath it: **1. "Settled" is a category error for this domain.** The question imports the drug-approval model: run the trial, adjudicate once, the claim stays true because the molecule stays the same molecule. A clinical AI claim has no such stability. Performance is a perishable, local fact — conditional on site, case mix, EHR documentation quirks, upstream data pipelines, the model version, and the workflow wrapped around it. The Epic sepsis model is the canonical case: strong claimed performance, then an external validation (Wong et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2021) found an AUC of ~0.63 at a real site, and the tool was eventually rebuilt. And even a *true* claim decays — populations drift, documentation practices change, the vendor ships version 4.2. So no quantity or quality of evidence can ever "settle" the claim in the sense the question imagines. The honest reframe is from **verdict to maintenance**: not "what evidence convinces us once?" but "what arrangement keeps the claim continuously true, and who is accountable when it stops being true?" Evidence still matters enormously — but as an ongoing process (silent-mode deployment, local validation, drift monitoring, decommissioning triggers), not as a one-time admission ticket. **2. "What should count" presupposes a counting-house.** For drugs, there's a place where evidence is weighed and has the power to say no. For most clinical AI as actually deployed — non-device CDS, EHR-vendor-bundled tools, in-house models — that place mostly doesn't exist. Tools enter care through procurement and vendor bundling, not through an evidentiary gate; your own question's premise ("enter practice on demos") concedes this. Adoption precedes evidence, routinely. So asking what evidence *should count* skips the prior institutional question: who counts it, at what moment, with what authority, and what happens after yes. That's why the question feels like it's missing the point — it's a normative question addressed to a referee who hasn't been hired. On your second question — what actually needs to change — I'd say the load-bearing items are institutional and infrastructural, and they differ by audience: - **Institutions** need verification capacity, not just standards: algorithm oversight committees with real authority (the P&T-committee analogue some systems now run), shadow-mode evaluation before activation as default practice, post-deployment monitoring, and — almost universally missing — predefined *kill criteria*. Everyone has an adoption process; almost no one has a decommissioning process. - **Clinicians** need calibrated reliance, not trust. The empirical reality is that clinicians don't adopt on evidence anyway — they adopt on workflow fit and defaults, and de-adopt on one salient bad case. What they lack is the information to know *when* the tool is in its competence zone and when to override, plus liability rules that don't punish them both for following it and for ignoring it. - **Patients** need disclosure and recourse more than they need trial results — knowing an algorithm was involved, and having somewhere to go when it fails. Notice that evidence regimes are embedded in all three, but never as the headline. So to answer you directly: "what kind of evidence" is genuinely *part* of what needs resolving — but it's downstream of "what keeps claims true over time" and "who has the standing to demand proof." An essay that answers the evidence question alone would re-derive consensus that already exists in the reporting-guideline literature (DECIDE-AI, CONSORT-AI, local-validation guidance) and miss the interesting part. If I were sharpening the essay question, I'd try something like: *"A clinical AI's accuracy is a perishable fact about a particular place and time — so performance claims can't be settled, only maintained. What has to exist for that maintenance to happen, and who answers when a claim quietly stops being true?"* That keeps your original question alive inside it (evidence is the raw material of maintenance) but relocates the weight to where you sense it belongs. One more reason to like the reframe, given where this would likely run: "claims that decay and the machinery that keeps them honest" is squarely a correspondence-over-time story — it's an Anchors & Ropes problem where the anchor itself moves. The original framing would have made it a policy explainer; the reframe makes it an epistemics essay, which is your lane.
