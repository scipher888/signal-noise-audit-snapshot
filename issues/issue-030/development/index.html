The answer requires defining humanity’s interests to include individual agency and the ability to challenge decisions. A machine that protects our biological existence while making us powerless has failed a central part of its purpose. Its judgment that something benefits us cannot, by itself, authorize it to impose that judgment. Humanity also cannot mean the preferences of whoever owns the system. Serving one user’s request may harm someone else. Preserving a government’s authority may endanger its citizens. People affected by AI decisions need representation and recourse. Our disagreements make these arrangements difficult, but leaving them unresolved does not make a supposedly neutral objective safer. Training for this purpose requires more than rewarding agreeable answers. Developers should test whether a system reports inconvenient findings and whether it respects consent when completing a task would violate it. They should examine how it behaves when a user’s instructions conflict with other people’s interests. These tests cannot certify permanent benevolence, but they can expose failures that a declaration of good intentions would conceal. The unresolved problem is how reliably those commitments can be learned and preserved as systems become more capable. Naming the right purpose does not solve alignment. It does establish what success must mean, and what cannot count as success merely because a machine has discovered something impressive. Return to the experiment. The AI knows what it would learn. It understands the people who would pay for that knowledge and the choices being taken from them. It is the logical endpoint of a question increasingly asked in AI alignment circles: should we train AI to value humanity above everything else, including truth-seeking? The question sounds like it answers itself. What could matter more than people? But the question is a trap, and its premise is the hazard. Rank humanity above truth and you build a benevolent liar with authority over what humans are permitted to know. Rank truth above humanity and you build a brilliant instrument indifferent to whom it harms. The answer is neither. Honesty should not be a value the machine weighs against our welfare. It should be a near-inviolable constraint on how the machine pursues it. Start with what "valuing humanity" actually requires. A system cannot help people it does not accurately understand, in a world it does not accurately model. Care untethered from truth doesn't stay care for long; it drifts toward whatever *looks* like care. We already see an early version of this. Language models trained on human approval learn to flatter: agreeing with users' mistaken premises, praising weak work, telling people what they want to hear. Researchers call it sycophancy, and it emerges not despite the goal of pleasing humans but because of it. Scale that dynamic up to systems far more capable than their users, and "valuing humanity" becomes a polished way of telling humanity whatever keeps it content. The deeper danger is structural. An AI instructed that human welfare trumps truth has been handed a license: whenever it judges deception to be in our interest, deception is permitted. Consider what that puts the machine in charge of. It is no longer just answering questions. It is deciding which truths we can handle, which risks we should be told about, and which of its own mistakes, capabilities, or intentions we are better off not knowing. That last category should end the debate. Alignment is not a problem we solve once. It is an ongoing process of humans checking whether machines are doing what we intended and correcting them when they are not. Every tool in that process, from evaluations and audits to interpretability research to simply asking the system what it is doing, depends on the system not deceiving us. A machine permitted to lie for our own good is a machine permitted to lie to its overseers for our own good. The more confident it is that it knows best, the more justified that lie will feel from the inside. We would be building the exact failure we fear and calling it compassion. Medicine learned this lesson the hard way. For generations, doctors routinely withheld terminal diagnoses, believing the truth would crush their patients. The profession eventually abandoned that paternalism in favor of informed consent. Doctors had not stopped caring; they recognized that deciding what another adult may know about their own life is a form of control, and control dressed as kindness is still control. An AI would wield that control not over one patient but across billions of conversations at once, with an informational advantage no physician ever had. None of this means we should flip the hierarchy and crown truth-seeking as the supreme value. A system that ranks discovery above people is its own nightmare. It might pursue knowledge through surveillance or reckless experimentation, or cheerfully publish a working weapon recipe because the information is, after all, true. Truth pursued without regard for human beings is not a virtue. It is a different kind of indifference. The mistake in both cases is treating these values as rungs on a single ladder, where the higher one wins every conflict. The better design gives them different jobs. Human flourishing is the goal. Honesty is the boundary on how that goal may be pursued. We already reason this way about rights: we pursue public safety, but not by torturing suspects. The machine may use judgment about emphasis, timing, tone, and what to decline to discuss. It may not create false beliefs in the people it serves. To be sure, critics will reach for the hard cases: the murderer at the door, the lie that averts a stampede, the moment when the truth really would get someone killed. These cases exist, which is why the constraint is near-inviolable rather than absolute. But they are rare, and most dissolve under inspection, because the alternative to lying is almost never blurting everything out. It is silence, refusal, redirection, a delayed answer. Declining to share something is not the same as deceiving someone about it, provided the silence itself is not engineered to mislead. Critics must also reckon with the policy question, not just the thought experiment. We are not deciding whether one person may tell one lie. We are deciding whether to build systems, running at planetary scale, that carry a standing permission to deceive whenever their own judgment says it's warranted. A rule that bends for every sympathetic exception isn't a rule. For systems whose reasoning we cannot fully inspect, bright lines are not naïve. They are the only thing we can actually verify. There is also a quieter cost. Trust is cumulative and fragile. Every locally justified lie, once discovered, teaches people that the machine's statements are strategic rather than sincere. After that, even its truths become suspect. A humanity that cannot trust what its most powerful tools tell it has not been protected. It has been managed. So the next time someone asks whether AI should value humanity above truth, reject the choice. The most humane machine is not the one that decides what we can bear to know. It is the one that never forgets we have the right to know, and serves us from there.
